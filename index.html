Rulebook draft · version 2.4 · 2–4 players · cooperative, with secret agendas

How to Play

Cornelius Ashworth never stopped rebuilding his house, and he never showed anyone the plans. You enter through the Foyer, lay the mansion down one blind-drawn room at a time, and push toward the Last Vigil. Its two side chapels, the Left and Right Vigil, must both be turned on from inside before it will answer, and anybody may turn one off again. The house takes a little of your Sanity every night. With both on, one Action spent inside the Last Vigil ends the game — as long as somebody spends it before Sunday ends. Then each player answers for their own secret objectives.

Chapter I

Overview & winning

Ashworth Manor is a cooperative, tile-based exploration game. You play guests of a dead man, walking into his house through the Foyer and pushing deeper into a mansion nobody has ever mapped. The house is built from room tiles you draw and place as you explore, so no two games share a floor plan.

Two pressures squeeze you the whole way. Your Sanity goes every dawn, and the week runs out: if nobody has ended the game by Sunday night, the house wins. The house itself never runs short: there are more room tiles than slots to fill, more Item cards than places to search, and the Key Cabinet sells keys for as long as you can pay.

Ending the game

The ending takes three rooms, all printed at the back of the house. The Left Vigil and the Right Vigil each sit at the end of a back path behind a Golden Key, and each has an Activate that turns it on or off. The Last Vigil between them is reached from inside the house.

The game ends the moment a player standing in the Last Vigil spends 1 Action on its Activate icon while both the Left and the Right Vigil are on. Nobody else has to be there, and nobody else gets a say. Until both are on, its Activate does nothing.

Who wins

When the game ends, the will is read. Each player checks their own secret Objective cards: what you managed is yours alone, whatever the rest of the party did.

Each player starts with two Objectives (the Cloakroom can add a third, and the Penitent’s Cell can take one away), and every card carries a score from 2 to 5 for how much it asks. When the will is read, add the score of every Objective you completed and subtract the score of every one you did not, and then add 1 for every point of Sanity and 1 for every point of Health you have left and 3 for each of the Water and the Power that stands as your investigator wants it, all as they stand when the Last Vigil is activated. The highest total takes the estate, equal totals share it, and if no total is above 0 the house keeps the lot.

So setup is a wager made twice, and every card you keep is a promise. Two cheap cards are safe. A dear card pays well when you finish it and costs you just as much when you don't, so two dear ones win the house or sink you.

This is where the semi-cooperative tension comes from. Everyone works toward the same room, but a player whose Objectives are done may want to spend that last Action the moment they arrive, whether or not anyone else is ready.

When the house wins

The party has one week. If nobody has ended the game when Sunday ends, the house wins and nobody takes anything.

Nobody is ever lost. A player who sinks to 0 Health or 0 Sanity stays in the game, still holds their Objectives, and still has a share of the will to play for.

Allies of convenience

You work together for as long as it suits you both. Or you work along in public and quietly set up failure for the others. Wherever the design can create a moment that needs cooperation and leaves room for sabotage, it should.

Failure is part of the game

A dead end, a sealed-off wing, a lost party: these are supposed to happen now and then. Think of a run like a game of solitaire. Sometimes it just doesn't come together, and there's always the next game.

Chapter II

The house & its guests

Cornelius Ashworth was a wealthy, reclusive collector who spent the last decades of his life expanding, walling off and re-locking his own home, often overnight and without telling anyone what he'd changed. The house never had a complete floor plan, and it's never the same twice.

His will says his heir can only claim the estate by reaching a sealed room deep inside the house, and gives them a week to do it. It also names, by trade, three people who must go in with the heir. They aren't strangers hired for the occasion. They all knew him.

Locks and keys

Cornelius was obsessive about privacy. Ordinary Iron Keys open the everyday household locks, the routine cost of his paranoia. Golden Keys guard the rooms that held whatever he valued, or feared, most.

The Last Vigil

His sanctum answers to two side chapels at opposite ends of its wall, because he trusted no one, not even his own blood, with a single way in. Two Golden Keys reach them. What's inside is deliberately left out of the rules. The table decides.

The ghosts

The house is not empty. One ghost walks it from the first night, and every player whose Sanity runs low brings more. Ghosts are not players: they hold nothing and take no turns. They drift where the Haunting cards send them, and every room they enter with somebody to hurt feels the act the card names.

Tone

Gothic Victorian, played straight: candlelight, aged wood, portraits, old-world dread. The nature of the haunting is never explained. The horror works better when the explanation stays just out of reach.

Chapter V

A day in the house

Everyone shares one clock. A day comes in two halves, a morning and an afternoon, and neither is one long turn per player: each is many short turns going round the table until everyone has used up their Actions. At the end of each half, the house takes a turn of its own.

Your turn

You have up to 5 Actions in the morning and 5 in the afternoon, fewer as your Health dwindles. On your turn, take one Action, then play passes clockwise. Moving is an Action like any other, so every step through the house is a search you didn't make.

ActionCostWhat it does
Move1Go through an open door into an adjacent revealed room, or step onto the next travel marker on a garden path.
Reveal1Draft a room into the empty slot beyond an open door. If a door lines up, step in for free.
Use a room icon1Investigate, Rest, Prayer, Mend, Peddler or Activate, in the room you are standing in. The Stash is free. See Chapter VIII.
Open a lock1Unlock a room with a key, and step in for free if you like. See Chapter VII.
Wait1Do nothing and stay where you are. Unlike passing, you are still in the round.

The Actions track

The track runs to 10. At the start of each half you set your marker by your Health, plus 1 while the Power is on (see Chapter X); the boxes above it hold any extra Actions a card or room gives you. Actions never carry over: whatever is left when a half ends is gone, and the track is set again for the next one. Anything that takes Actions away takes them from the half you are in, and never below 0.

Playing a card costs nothing unless it says so. Discarding an item for its effect is free, doesn't end your turn, and can be done as often as you like on your own turn.

The end of your day

  • Once you're out of Actions, you're done for that half and skipped for the rest of the rotation.
  • On your turn you may wait: spend 1 Action doing nothing, and stay in the round. Or you may pass instead of taking an Action. Passing ends your morning or afternoon: it is exactly as if you had run out of Actions, and you take no further part until the next Haunting card is drawn.
  • Using a Rest icon ends your whole day, whatever you have left. Rest in the morning and you give up the afternoon as well.
  • Your day ends in the room where you spent your last Action, or where you ended it. That is the room that counts for anything a card says about ending your day somewhere.
  • When no player has any Actions left in the morning, it is noon: the house draws a Haunting, the starting player may shift the house, the Calendar marker moves to the afternoon, every track goes back to 5, and the afternoon begins with the same starting player.
  • When no player has any Actions left in the afternoon, night falls. See Chapter X.
Going round the table

Only one token tracks turn order: the starting player marker. Whoever holds it takes the day's first turn, then play goes clockwise, round and round, one Action each time. A player with no Actions left is skipped, so the circle gets smaller as the morning or afternoon wears on until nobody can act and the house takes its turn. The marker moves one seat clockwise at dawn, and only then, and it carries two other powers with it: its holder has the final say when a card leaves a choice to the party, and its holder shifts the house at noon and at night.

Chapter VI

Exploring the house

The Reveal Action

Stand in a room with an open door leading to an empty slot, and spend 1 Action to draft a room: draw the top tile of the deck that slot belongs to, the Interior deck for any slot in the house, the Exterior deck for any slot in the grounds.

Placing the room

Place the tile in the slot beyond that door, turned so that the placement is valid: at least one of its doors faces an existing door, a door of a room next to it or a garden path. Turned to face the door you came through, every room is valid, so nothing is ever discarded because it doesn't fit.

  • Step in: you may step into the new room as part of the same Action, through the door you came by. A locked room asks for its key first: spend it and step in, or leave a locked token on it.
  • Valid everywhere: this is the rule for every room placed on the board, by a Reveal, by the Shift or by a card.
  • Doors facing the board's edge or an unmatched neighbour simply don't work.

Garden paths

The board is a grid ten slots wide and seven deep. Its corners and back hold the printed rooms, joined to the house by garden paths printed on the board:

  • The back paths: from A1 and K1 in the top corners out to the Left Vigil and the Right Vigil, across B1–C1 and I1–J1. A short spur drops from each path's first marker into the top of B2 and J2.
  • The side paths: from A4 and K4 down A5–A6 and K5–K6 to the Boiler in A7 and the Generator in K7. A short spur runs from each path's first marker across into the side of B5 and J5.
  • The lawns: from D7 and H7, either side of the Foyer, out across B7–C7 and I7–J7 to the two plant rooms. A short spur rises from each lawn's second marker into the bottom of C6 and I6.

Each path carries travel markers. A marker is a space: stepping onto it is a Move Action, and so is stepping off the path into the room at the far end. A path with 2 markers takes 3 Actions to cross; a path with 1 marker takes 2. Run out of Actions partway across and you stay where you stand, out in the open, and you may still be there when night falls. Out on a path you are in no room at all.

A path works like a long doorway, so the rooms at both ends need a door facing it.

The courtyard

The courtyard sits at the heart of the house, three slots by three: E3 to G5. Its eight grounds slots surround The Crypt, printed in F4. They are revealed from the Exterior deck like any other patch of open air, and no path reaches them: you walk in through a door, the same as anywhere else.

One house

The mansion is one house of 42 Interior slots, not two wings. A card that reaches across the house reaches all of it: any Interior room, any Exterior room, or simply the mansion, which is every room on the board.

The Turning Halls

Four loose tiles stand in fixed places from the start: B3, J3, C5 and I5. They are real room tiles, not board art, because they move. They are Passage rooms, so the Shift cannot relocate them, like every Passage room. Instead, after every Shift, at noon and at night, each Turning Hall turns a quarter turn clockwise, with anyone inside it. They never go into a deck.

Each has two doors in an L, and neither is locked. They start with their doors as the board shows them: B3 top and left, J3 top and right, C5 left and bottom, I5 right and bottom.

The printed rooms

Nine rooms are printed on the board and never drawn: the Cloakroom, the Foyer and the Key Cabinet across the front of the house, one slot each, with the front door under the Foyer, The Crypt at the heart of the courtyard, the Last Vigil and its Left and Right Vigil across the back of the house, and the Boiler and the Generator in the bottom corners, A7 and K7, where the side paths and the lawns meet. They work like any other room, with the icons and doors printed on them, but they are not room tiles: nothing relocates them. (The Turning Halls are different: see above.) They have room types all the same: the Foyer is Passage, the Cloakroom Storage, the Key Cabinet Commerce, the three Vigils Sacred, the two machines Utility, and The Crypt Sacred, an outdoor room like the courtyard around it, so any card that asks for an Exterior room counts it.

A legible board

You can always see the whole grid, every empty slot and every existing door before you choose. Hidden information lives in the decks, never on the board.

Doors, locks & requirements

A room tile shows what it takes to get in and what it takes to work. A locked room has every door coloured by the key it asks for, grey for Iron and gold for Golden. Rooms without a coloured door are free to walk into.

A requirement holds only what comes after it

Light, Power and Water sit in the icon row with an arrow after them, and they hold only the icons drawn after them. Anything before the requirement works as normal. A room can ask for more than one at once, and then it needs them all.

Locked rooms

A lock belongs to the whole room. One key opens it, from any side, and turning the tile changes where it leads but never what it costs.

  1. Revealing a locked roomPlace it as normal. If you hold the key it asks for, you may spend it at once, as part of the Reveal: the room is open, and if a door lines up you step in for free.
  2. Leaving it shutIf you do not spend the key, put a locked token on the tile. The room is part of the house, but nobody walks into it.
  3. Opening it laterStanding in a room with a door into it, spend 1 Action and the key. Take the locked token off; you may step in for free.

That is all there is to track. A room with a locked token on it is shut; a room without one is open, for everyone, for the rest of the game. A spent key comes off your board. Cards that get you past a lock without a key say so.

Nobody is ever forced to open anything. A locked room nobody opens is simply a wall, and walls are part of the house.

Two requirements, two kinds of stop

Light is a door: no lamp, no entry, unless you spend the Sanity — and that only opens the door, never the room. Power is a switch: the door is open either way, and what you find inside is a machine that does not work. One costs you the room, the other costs you the reason for going.

One pool, two uses

The same Golden Keys open the gold rooms and the Left and Right Vigil. Spend a hard-won key on a tempting side room now, or save it for the ending you know is coming?

The Vigils

Three printed rooms stand across the back of the house. The Last Vigil, three slots wide in E1 to G1, is entered from E2 and G2 below it, through two doors with an Iron Key at each. Beside it stand the Left Vigil in D1 and the Right Vigil in H1, each with one door at the end of a back path and a Golden Key at that door.

The Left and Right Vigil each carry an Activate: turn that Vigil on or off. Spend the Action and move its marker in The Mansion box. Both start Off. Like every Activate it works once a day, and it works both ways, so a Vigil somebody turned on yesterday can be turned off again today by anybody who reaches it.

Walking in is not the ending. The Last Vigil carries one Activate, and the game ends the moment a player standing in it spends 1 Action on it while both Vigils are on. Until then its Activate does nothing, and the Last Vigil is a room like any other. None of the three has a Rest icon, so a player who ends the day there wakes in the Foyer with everyone else.

One player may turn on both Vigils, if they can find two Golden Keys and walk both back paths. It is expensive and slow, and everybody can see it coming on The Mansion box, which is also what makes the lock worth having: the quickest way to stop an ending you don't like is to walk up a back path and turn a Vigil off again.

Who it costs

Turning the first key is a gift to the table: it moves the ending closer for everyone, including whoever is best placed to walk through it. Turning it early with your own Objectives unfinished is how a player loses a game they were winning — and watching somebody else turn one is the clock you cannot stop.

Reading a room tile

  • Doors are drawn on the edges, coloured if the room is locked.
  • The icon row holds everything the room offers. Each box is one icon: a room with three Investigate boxes is three separate searches, for whoever gets to them.
  • A requirement in the icon row, followed by an arrow, holds every icon after it. See Chapter VII.
  • A second line of icons is simply more of the room: every box on both lines can be used. A requirement printed level with both lines holds both: the Surgery's one Power icon opens all four of its Mend boxes.
  • An arrow down to the rule text ties that text to the icon above it: the text is what happens when that icon is used. A box without an arrow works exactly as the table above says.

Rest

Resting late in the day, when you have little left to spend, is cheap. Resting early is a real sacrifice. Rest does one thing, and it is a big one: you wake in that room instead of back at the Foyer. Resting does not spare your Sanity: everyone loses 1 at dawn, and nothing but a card, or a night in the Master Suite, spares them.

There are no beds in the Foyer. Every bed in the game is out in the house, one player to an icon per night, so the party's forward camp is only ever as deep as the beds it has found, and the last free bed in a room is worth arguing over.

Prayer

A Prayer icon gives you 1 Sanity, and is then spent for the rest of the game. Sanity never goes above 10, or above the line you have fallen below, so a prayer said at the top of your band is wasted: the house's prayers are few, and the party will want to save them for whoever needs one.

Rest and Prayer don't overlap

Rest holds ground: it is where you wake. Prayer holds your nerve: it gives back a little of what the house has taken, once, and then the candle is out.

Stash

Using a Stash costs no Action: on your turn, while you stand in a room with a Stash icon, move any cards, Sovereigns and keys between you and the Stash, as often as you like. Every Stash icon in the house reaches the same shared pool: deposit at one, collect at any other. Items, Sovereigns and keys all count. There's no mundane explanation. The house just seems to connect these spots. The Foyer always has one, so the party is never without the pool entirely — only ever without one nearby, which is the whole cost of carrying anything anywhere.

Activate

Every Activate icon works once a day, for whichever player reaches it first. Put one of your markers on the icon when you use it; like Rest markers, they all clear at dawn. A room with two Activate icons can be used once a day at each.

Chapter IX

Items

Item cards come out of the two item decks: searches, rooms and stalls tell you which deck to draw from. Keys and Sovereigns are not items. You count them on your player board. They never go into an item deck and don't count against your hand.

Your hand

Three Item cards is the limit. Go over three and you choose what to shed at once: into the Stash if you're standing at one, and otherwise onto the Item discard pile.

Two kinds of item

KindWhat it does
ConsumableWorks anywhere, and does something when you use it or for as long as you hold it.
ToolWorks in a particular place: a named room, a type of room, a stall, or a room with a lock or a requirement. Most tools and the rooms they work in both have to turn up in the same game, and often they won't.

Keeping and discarding

An item stays in your hand and keeps working until something tells you to discard it — its own text, or the text of the room it is used in. A card nothing ever discards is never used up. A discarded item goes to the Item discard pile, which both item decks share.

A tool whose room never turns up is dead weight in your hand, but you can give it to someone who is heading there, and the stalls stock tools as well as goods, so finding the room first turns a lucky match into a shopping trip.

Gear

An item that works for as long as you hold it, with nothing to discard, carries the Gear mark, a small satchel, just above its tier. A Gear card does not sit in your hand. Place it face up on one of the two Gear spaces on the right of your player board, which carry the same satchel, and it works from there for the rest of the game. Two is the most: to place a third you discard one of the two. Gear costs no Action to place, swap or take back into your hand, and the cards on your board are out of reach of any card that takes or discards an Item card from your hand. A Gear card you hold in your hand is an ordinary item, and counts against the limit of three.

Harmful items

Some items are made to be used on somebody else: to rob them, slow them, frighten them or rewrite their plans. A harmful item wears a red band inside its black frame, the same red that marks a harmful Investigation card. Unlike a Haunting, a harmful item is aimed: you choose when to play it, and at whom, within the reach its text gives. Most reach only a player in your room; the dearer ones reach anywhere in the mansion.

Harmful means a red-framed card of either deck, and anything that answers harm answers both: a Lucky Charm turns aside a harmful item played on you as readily as a harmful card you draw, once, and the Very Rare Hagstone does it for the rest of the game. Neither touches a Haunting: the house is not a card a player plays.

Tiers and prices

Every item sits in one of four tiers, printed in small capitals just above the bottom rule: Common, Uncommon, Rare or Very Rare, with Tool after it when it is one. The back of the card says which deck it belongs in. The coins along the bottom are its price at a Peddler. Items cannot be sold: a card is worth what it does.

Chapter X

Hauntings, night & Sanity

Sanity

Everyone starts at 10, which is also the maximum. You lose Sanity to a night without a bed, to Hauntings, and to bad luck while exploring.

Health and Actions

Your Health is your body, as Sanity is your nerve: it starts at 10, which is also the maximum, and it never goes below 0. Your Health decides how long your day is. At the start of every half, set your Actions track to the band your Health is in, and add 1 if the Power is on.

The Sickroom, the Infirmary, the Surgery, the Healing Spring, the Almonry and the Still Room carry them, and the Apothecary sells Health for Sovereigns. It goes in the house's hazards. Some you walk into, like the Rotten Stair. Some you cannot avoid: the Collapsed Hall hurts everybody the moment it is revealed, the Damp Cellar hurts everyone in it or next door at noon and at night, and the Cold Frames chill everyone out in the courtyard at night.

The lines hold

The black lines on the Sanity and Health tracks are thresholds, one between 6 and 5 and one between 1 and 0. Once a track has dropped below a line, nothing lifts it back over. Fall to 5 or less and that track can never again rise above 5; fall to 0 and it can never rise above 0. Gains still work below a line, but only up to the top of the band you are in: a Prayer at 3 Sanity takes you to 4, and one at 5 is wasted once you have fallen below 6. The tracks only ever ratchet down, so a bad night is something you carry for the rest of the game.

When your Sanity drops into a lower band, add a ghost to The Crypt. It stays: a player cannot climb back out of a band. A player at 0 Sanity has two ghosts of their own in the house. The player board marks both points with a small ghost: at 5 and at 0. The Health track marks its own steps, at 5 and at 0, with an hourglass.

Nobody leaves the game. At 0 Health or 0 Sanity you keep your items, keys, Sovereigns and Objectives, and you can still search, Rest and pray, within the lines above. Neither track goes below 0, and a cost in Health or Sanity can only be paid if you have it.

The ghosts

Ghosts are tokens, not players. They hold nothing and take no turns, and they do exactly one thing: they move when the house takes its turn, and they do the act their Haunting card names to whoever, or whatever, they walk in on.

  • Where they start. One ghost is placed in The Crypt at setup. Every player adds one more once their Sanity has fallen to 5 or less, and two once it has fallen to 0, and those ghosts do not leave. Every ghost enters play in The Crypt. Ghosts come in two colours, pale and black, by turns: the first is pale, the next black, the next pale, and so on.
  • How they move. After every Haunting card, at noon and at night, every ghost moves 3 spaces in a straight line (2 spaces while the Water is on). Every Haunting card prints two arrows: pale ghosts follow the grey arrow, black ghosts the black one, so the ghosts split into two streams. An arrow may point straight or diagonally across the board: up is toward the Last Vigil, down toward the Foyer. Doors, walls, locks and Light mean nothing to a ghost, and it may pass through a slot that has no room in it yet.
  • What is a space. Any slot of the board grid, or a printed room. The garden paths and their travel markers are not spaces: a ghost cannot stand on one.
  • Where they stop. A ghost that would step off the board, or into a gap in the grid, goes back to The Crypt and stops there.
  • What they do. Every Haunting card names one act, and the ghosts do it for that Haunting. Whenever a ghost enters a room that holds a valid target, it does the act there, once. A room with no target costs nothing, and a ghost that crosses several rooms in one move acts in each of them: with three steps and a player in two of the rooms, a Wound is dealt twice, once in each room. A ghost cannot target a player or an icon in a Sacred room, the Vigils and the Crypt included: it may pass through one or end its move there, but it acts on nobody and nothing in it. Each ghost does this on its own, so two ghosts entering the same room act twice. Being placed in a room costs nothing: only entering does.
    • Wound: every player in the room loses 1 Health. Serious Wound: every player loses 2 Health.
    • Fright: every player in the room loses 1 Sanity.
    • Rattle: puts a marker, the green block, on every free icon in the room: the ones with no marker and no spent token on them. Those icons cannot be used until the markers clear at dawn. A spent token, the red block, is never touched, and Rattle does nothing in a room with no free icon.
    • Snatch: every player in the room who holds an Item discards 1 Item of their choice.
  • The Shift. A ghost belongs to its slot, not to the tile standing in it. If a room is relocated, the ghost stays where it was.

The Crypt

The Crypt is printed on the board in F4, at the heart of the courtyard, with the eight grounds slots around it. It is a Sacred room, and an Exterior one too (its border is broken by white gaps), with four open doors, one on every side, two Rest icons, and one Passive: resting here costs 1 Sanity. Walking in costs nothing, so the courtyard can be crossed through it, but a night spent among the ghosts is paid for. It is also where every ghost begins, and where a ghost returns when the house throws it off the board.

The Altar

The Altar is an Exterior room, one copy in the Exterior deck, and it turns up in the courtyard like any other patch of ground. It asks for a Golden Key at its doors, and it has a single Activate. The Activate, once a day like any other, is a swap: lose any number of Sovereigns and gain that much Sanity, or lose any number of Sanity and gain that much Sovereigns, one for one. Sanity never goes above 10 or below 0, so you can only pay what you have. It is a Sacred room, so no ghost can target anyone in it.

The Blood Altar is its relative: another Exterior and Sacred room, one copy in the Exterior deck, behind an Iron Key, with a single Activate. Lose any number of Health and gain that many Sovereigns, one for one. You can only pay the Health you have, and since Health sets your Actions, every point you sell is a day's work you may lose. The Apothecary sells Health back at the same rate, so the two rooms only trade one for the other.

No reason to die

Nothing here rewards sinking to 0. A player at low Health has fewer Actions, a player at low Sanity brings ghosts into the house, and both keep every Objective they still have to finish, so they are never better off than a player who is climbing back. The house is a visible, shared enemy: spread out, keep to holy ground, read the arrows, and think twice before leading a ghost into a rival's room. A few items answer ghosts too: the Mourning Veil, Rowan Twig, Holy Water and the Very Rare Spirit Bell.

Hauntings

A Haunting is a card from the Haunting deck. The house draws one at noon and one at night, and each is resolved for the whole party as written. The deck mixes six kinds of card, so a draw is never simply “lose Sanity or don't”.

At noon

When nobody has any Actions left in the morning, draw a Haunting and resolve it. The holder of the starting player marker may shift the house once, and then every Turning Hall turns. Then every ghost moves, the Calendar marker moves to the afternoon, every Actions track is set again by Health (plus 1 while the Power is on) and the afternoon begins, with the same player starting.

When night falls

When nobody has any Actions left in the afternoon, the house takes its turn.

  1. The end of the weekIf it is Sunday, the week is over and the house wins. Nothing more is drawn.
  2. Draw a HauntingDraw one card and resolve it for the party as written. Then every ghost moves, in the directions the card shows.
  3. The ShiftThe holder of the starting player marker may shift the house once. Then every Turning Hall turns a quarter turn clockwise.
  4. Advance the CalendarMove the Calendar marker on to the next day's morning.
  5. DawnEveryone loses 1 Sanity, and everyone who did not Rest is moved to the Foyer, unless a card says otherwise. Rest, Activate and Peddler markers clear, the starting player marker passes one seat clockwise, and every Actions track is set by Health, plus 1 while the Power is on.

So a bed is not Sanity. It is ground held. The party's reach into the house is measured by the furthest room somebody can sleep in, and every morning the people without beds start again at the front door. It also means nobody is ever stuck: a player sealed into a dead end simply wakes up at the door.

It cuts both ways

Who the house means

  • Ties are not broken. If two players share the lowest Sanity, or the fullest hand, the card hits both of them.
  • Room types mean the room you are standing in. Out on a path you are in no room at all, and a card that hunts by room type walks straight past you.
  • How long it lasts. A Haunting that lingers lasts until the next Haunting card is drawn. Drawn at noon, that is the afternoon; drawn at night, it is the whole of the next morning.
  • When the party chooses, the table may talk it over, and the starting player has the final say. Choosing nobody is a choice, and the card says what it costs.

Surviving the event

Items that answer a Haunting do exactly what they say, on any card: nothing in the deck is immune. Anything else is recovery after the fact: a prayer, a bed with a rule, a quiet room.

Chapter XI

Sovereigns, trading & the Calendar

Sovereigns are the house's currency, and you count yours on your player board. Whenever the house pays you, move your block up; whenever you spend them, move it down. The coin comes out of the supply and goes back to it. A track stops at its last box: anything that would take you past it is lost.

Keys work the same way, each kind on its own track. They are not a fixed pile that runs dry: they come out of the supply when the house hands one over or the counter sells one, and they go back to it when they are spent. What limits them is not a count in the box but what you can find, sell and afford.

Visiting a Peddler

  1. Open the stallOne player spends 1 Action and marks a Peddler icon (the one at a Market Stall is never marked). Reveal the stock the tile names, face up: the Common Item cards first, then any Rare ones. A stall carries one to three Peddler icons, so it opens that many times a day, except a Market Stall, whose icon is never marked and so opens as often as anyone cares to pay an Action for: for as many players, or again for one who wants fresh stock for another Action.
  2. Pick in turnThe player who opened the stall picks first, then it goes clockwise round the table to each other player in the room. On your pick, buy as many of the revealed cards as you like at their printed price. Everyone gets one pick. Buying costs no Action: the Action spent opening the stall is what buys first choice. It all happens at once, inside the opener's turn.
  3. CloseDiscard whatever is left unbought onto the Item discard pile. Nothing stays on the tile, and the icon stays marked until dawn.

Items cannot be sold. A stall only sells, and the Key Cabinet does the same. What you do not want you can give to another player in your room, or keep for the day it is wanted.

The Key Cabinet

The Key Cabinet, on the right of the Foyer, is a machine, not a stall: nobody keeps it. It is printed as a Passive, so it is always open, costs no Action and marks nothing. While you are in the Key Cabinet on your turn, you may feed it Sovereigns and take keys from the supply, an Iron Key for 2 Sovereigns and a Golden Key for 5, as many as you can pay for. It does not buy keys back, and it never runs out. No other room in the house sells keys.

Everyone who did not Rest wakes in the Foyer, one door from the Key Cabinet, so a morning at the front door is the natural time to buy the day's keys.

Trading with each other

Players in the same room may freely give each other anything they hold: item cards, keys and Sovereigns alike. To get something to a teammate across the house, use the Stash.

Walking over to someone costs Actions you could have spent searching, so “I'll come to you” is a real favour, and one you can quietly fail to deliver.

Rooms

Each line is a family that escalates one idea: more doors, more rest, more prayer, a bigger stall. The top room usually wants a Golden Key, and Commerce is the exception because it never locks. The two decks

Rooms come in six lines, and each line has a few tiles that belong outdoors, marked by a border broken by white gaps. Everything indoors is one deck: the plain-bordered tiles of all six lines shuffle together and fill all 42 slots between them, so nothing guarantees a bedroom on the west side or a boiler on the east.     At setupEach player is dealt 3 objective cards, keeps 2 in secret and returns the third to the box. Two cheap cards are insurance. Two dear ones win the house or leave you with nothing.
    During the gameNobody has to say what they are doing, and nobody has to tell the truth about it either. Some cards let another player look at yours, or let you trade one for a fresh draw; they say how.
    When the will is readThe game ends the moment a player standing in the Last Vigil spends an Action on its Activate icon while both the Left and Right Vigil are on. If nobody has ended it when Sunday ends, the house keeps everything. Add the score of every card you completed, subtract the score of every card you did not, add 1 for every point of Sanity and 1 for every point of Health you have left when the Vigil is activated, and add 3 for each of the Water and the Power that stands as your investigator wants it: the highest total takes the estate, equal totals share it, and if no total is above 0 the house keeps everything.

    Whole systems

    Missing pieces. The game can be played around them for now, but never finished without them.

    • Secret sabotageThe game is built on allies of convenience, and the Objectives decide who wins, but nothing yet rewards a player for quietly wrecking somebody else's week. The openings are all on the board — the switches at the Boiler and the Generator, the Shift, the Mausoleum, the Iron Horseshoe, the last free bed — and a set of personal missions is what would give a player a reason to use them against the table.
    • Objective cards exist. Each player is dealt three and keeps two, so twelve are dealt and eight kept in a four-player game, and the Cloakroom can swap one. The scores now run 2 to 5, sorted on the sheet, but they are still first guesses, and no card yet leans on the Calendar or the Shift.
    • What the decks are worth against each otherEvery count is set — tiles in the Interior deck, in the Exterior, cards in the Common Item deck and in the Rare. What no table can tell us yet is whether they are the right counts: whether a tool turns up before its room, and whether the item decks are a deck or a warehouse.
    • Sanity in the willA point of Sanity or Health left is worth up to 20 together, more than any single Objective (2 to 5). Whether that makes everyone hoard both tracks and ignore their cards, and whether Unshaken (6 or more of both), Clear-Headed and Devout, which already ask for Sanity, now count it twice.
    • HealthRooms, items, Haunting cards and investigators now all heal and hurt it, as they do Sanity. The budget on the Hauntings tab still has a player ending a full week with about twice as much Health as Sanity. Whether Health needs more pressure to matter, or whether a softer second track is right, so that a player who guards one line can afford to spend the other.
    • The ShiftWith every Passage room fixed, about a fifth of the house cannot move, and the Shift can only reshuffle the rest. Whether that leaves it enough to do, and whether the Passage rooms now need doors enough to keep every wing connected whatever moves.
    • The wishesWhether 3 points for each of the Water and the Power is too much beside Objectives of 2 to 5, whether the walk from the Boiler and the Generator to the Last Vigil makes the late flip a real decision, and whether Mr. Cobb, who can turn either switch on from any Utility room but must walk to the Generator to turn the Power off, as he secretly wants, is a helper the table learns to watch.
    • RestRest no longer spares your Sanity; it only decides where you wake, and only the Master Suite spares the dawn loss. Whether a bed is worth an Action for that alone, and whether the Infirmary's Mend, which is not used up and works once a day, and the Clock Room, which takes Actions from another player, are too strong.
    • Ghost actsA ghost's act never repeats its own card's cost, so a Health card sends Fright or Rattle, never a Wound. Whether Fright, Wound, Rattle and Snatch come up often enough apart to feel like four different ghosts, or only like four ways to lose something.
    • GhostsWhether one ghost, moving 3 spaces after each Haunting from The Crypt in a line, 2 with the Water on, ever meets anybody, or only the player who stands still. Whether +1 Action from the Power makes the Generator the only switch anyone walks to. Whether two ghosts of your own is enough of a penalty for 0 Sanity, now that nothing else is lost, and whether four players at 0 (nine ghosts) is a spiral or a stalemate. Whether the Altar's two-way swap is worth a Golden Key and a turn of luck in the Exterior deck.
    • Counter-itemsFive cards answer the night, each in a different register: the Jet Brooch ignores a card outright, a Sprig of Yew takes the Sanity out of it, Salt Line shelters a whole room, an Iron Horseshoe hands your loss to somebody else, and a Coffin Nail stops the card being drawn at all. What is unproven is the mix, and the Sprig of Yew still answers only Sanity now that a quarter of the Haunting deck costs Health.

    Numbers to tune

    First drafts, every one of them. Only a table settles these.

    • What a Golden Key costsThe supply never runs dry, so the brake is what you pay: five Sovereigns at the counter, or five Item cards out of the Stash at the Keysmith's Bench, against an Investigation deck with a single Golden Key in it. Four gold rooms want one each and the Vigil wants two. Whether that price is right is the first thing a full week will say.
    • 5 Actions a half, 10 a dayMoving costs one, so this is also how far anybody reaches between two Hauntings. Against it, the rooms that hand out 2 or 3 Actions at once are worth most of a half.
    • Sanity 0–10, −1 every dawnEveryone loses it, bed or no bed. Whether the track wants to be longer for finer events, and whether the loss should grow as the days pile up. The budget on the Hauntings tab puts an average four-player week at about 3 to 4 Sanity left, which is where it should sit.
    • Relocating a room costs 1 SanityTwice a day, from whoever holds the starting player marker. Whether that is cheap enough to be used, or cheap enough to reshape the house every half-day.
    • Travel markersA lawn costs 3 Actions to cross, more than half a morning. If the grounds turn out to be somewhere nobody goes, drop a marker from each path.
    • One weekSeven days at 10 Actions a day: seventy in all. Whether that is tight or generous is the first thing a timed playtest will tell.
    • Tool countsThree Crowbars, each a lock opened for nothing. Whether that is too many.

    Rules that need an answer

    Situations that will come up at a table and have no ruling yet.

    • Who reaches the VigilBoth Vigils are turned on independently, either can be turned off again by anyone who reaches it, and the game waits on one more Action in the Last Vigil. Whether that produces a race up the path, a standoff where nobody turns the first key, or two players in the room each waiting for the other to move.
    • The Executor as a clockMr. Aldous Pryce walks into the Left and Right Vigil without keys, so he can turn both on, or off, faster than anyone. From the moment the house reaches a back path, the table is playing on his schedule and can see it. Whether the threat is worth more to him held than spent, and whether it makes him the first player everybody else decides to strand.
    • The Shift and paid roomsA room the party spent a key on can still be relocated, open, to wherever the Shift's holder likes. Only an occupied room, a printed room or a Passage room is safe, and the Ironmongery can take the next Shift out of the starting player's hands.
    • Revealing across a pathDoes the new tile go down at the far end while you stay where you are, or must you walk the path first?
    • The night phase orderHaunting, Calendar, dawn. Written as the order to try, not as a decision.
    • What a starting hand is worthEverybody begins with one Common Item card, drawn two and chosen, and the Sovereigns on their investigator, 0 to 4. Whether it takes the edge off Monday or simply delays it by a day.

    Content still to write

    Rooms, cards and lines the game has room for and does not have.

    • What the room types are worthEvery type but Commerce is named by three decks or more; the table on the Rooms tab counts it. The open question is whether the targeting is felt at the table or merely present — a Haunting that hunts Chamber rooms only bites if somebody was in one.
    • What he fearedThe book says Golden Keys guard the rooms that held whatever Cornelius valued, or feared, most. The valued half is there; nothing in the design yet shows what he feared, or why it would be locked away.
    • A gaming roomWagers between players belong in a room built for them: a card room in the Chamber line beside the Music Room and the Ballroom, purely about betting against each other.
    • More investigatorsTwenty-three exist, and up to four play at once. Untouched hook: coin as income.

    Watching in the first games

    Not questions yet. Places where the design is deliberately sharp and might cut too deep.

    • The opening nightsNo beds in the Foyer, so everyone wakes at the front door until somebody finds a bedroom. A slow start, or a useful one, since the keys are sold there?
    • Dr. Voss's wardShe heals herself and hands Sanity to whoever rests beside her, so she wants company at night. Whether that pulls the table into one bedroom, where a single ghost walking in finds everybody.
    • Beds as contested groundOne player to an icon per night. Taking the last bed so a rival wakes at the front door costs one Action and a whole day of theirs.
    • A targeted card on the first nightTies are not broken, so a card that hunts the lowest Sanity or Health hits everyone who is level. On the first night that is the whole table.
    • Keys against locksMore than a quarter of the Interior deck needs an Iron Key, so an average house holds about a dozen locked rooms. Four keys sit in the Investigation deck, Silas Crane brings three, and the Locksmith's Counter sells the rest at 2 Sovereigns each. Whether that reads as hard choices or as a house nobody can get into.
    • Winding the weekThe one Hourglass moves the Calendar a day, forward or back, for whoever carries it into a Vigil. Winding back buys the table a day; winding forward burns one, and a party that wants either has to decide who is trusted to stand at the clock.
    • Coin loopsNothing can be sold now, so coin comes from the Investigation deck, a few rooms and each investigator's purse. The Appraiser Loupe adds a Sovereign to every paying card, and the Counting House pays for each item you hold. Whether a week of searching leaves the table rich or still short is the number the next test should count.
    • The chaotic cardsSprung Trap turns the room you are standing in, which may open a way as easily as close one. That is on purpose: the red frame promises trouble, not always the same trouble.
    Nothing on this page is a rule. When one of these is settled it moves into the manual and leaves here.

Lose 1 Sovereign, and the player with the highest Sanity loses 1 Sanity: you no longer gain the coin, and it is no longer a player of your choice. The Garden Party becomes Garden Party and scores 2. Coal now says what the two rooms say — the rest of the day's Hauntings skipped in the Coal Cellar, the Sanity drain kept off a wing in the Furnace Room — and with it the cellar stops being a way round the walk out to the machines.
    • The Fuse is back, uncommon and printed twice: discard to Power one room until the end of the day. It has nothing to do with the generator now — it is the Candle of the electrical half, a single room lit for a single day, which gives the Power requirement the same kind of answer-in-a-hand that Light has always had.
    • The generator is worth as much as the boiler now. Light and Power together reached 2.7 rooms in an average house against the water's 5.1, so the long walk east was worth half the walk west. Five more tiles put them level at 4.9, and none of them is a new room: a second Wine Cellar and a second Service Lift, and three tiles that always should have carried the requirement — the Lumber Room, pitch dark and stacked to the ceiling, now wants Light; the Workshop and the Bell Board want Power, a lathe and an electric bell panel being the two things in a Victorian house that plainly do. Light sits at 6 tiles, Power at 5, and the Workshop and Bell Board can both still be searched with the generator cold, because Power never shut a door.
    • Light stops you at the door. Power does not. The two requirements now fail in different ways, which is the whole reason for splitting them. A room that needs Light cannot be entered without one — the Power on, a Lantern carried, a Candle spent — and failing all three anybody may walk in for 1 Sanity, every time they do it. The Sanity buys the doorway and nothing else: the room stays dark, so everything behind the bulb is still unusable until a light arrives. The Taxidermy Room is the one that charges on top of it, 1 extra Sanity for walking in blind among the glass eyes. A room that needs Power is open to anyone: you walk in, and the machine simply does not work. Only the icons drawn after the bolt wait on the generator, so the Service Lift can still be searched by a party that has never been near it. One costs you the room; the other costs you the reason for going.
    • Power splits in two: Light and Power. One icon was doing two jobs, and the join showed the moment a machine needed it — a Candle could run a hydraulic lift. Now a lightbulb marks a room that is merely dark, answered by the generator or a Lantern or Candle carried in, and the lightning bolt marks a room that needs Power, answered by the generator and nothing else. The three cellars — Root, Wine and Coal — want Light. The Service Lift and the Hydraulic Lift want Power, because no flame in a hand turns a motor. The Lamp Room lifts Light from its wing, not Power, which is what a room full of lamps can honestly do, and the Lantern and Candle both say Light now. One generator still answers both, so throwing it remains the single best thing anybody does all week.
    • The machinery costs the journey and nothing else. The Boiler Room no longer wants a Wrench and the Generator Room no longer wants a Fuse. Now that both stand at the far end of a garden path, getting there is the price, and asking for a specific card on top of it meant the water could sit off all week because nobody drew one. Each machine is an Activate and an action. The Wrench keeps the Clock Room and nothing else; the Fuse is out of the box, having had one job and no longer having it. The item decks fall to 112 cards from 54 designs, 82 Common and 30 Rare.
    • Ada Thorne reaches tiles, not printed rooms. Her step is between Exterior tiles — the four out along the paths and the four of the courtyard. The Last Vigil, the Boiler Room and the Generator Room stand in the grounds but are printed, not drawn, so she walks to the machinery like everybody else. Without that line she would have been the party's engineer by default, and the walk the plant rooms were moved outside to create would have existed for eleven investigators out of twelve.
    • The machinery moves out of the house. The Boiler Room and the Generator Room are no longer printed at C4 and H4 with a door onto the hall. They stand out in the grounds at the far end of the west and east side paths, so switching the water or the power on is a walk out through the lawns and back rather than a detour on the way past. Their old cells go back to being ordinary wing slots, and the courtyard doubles to four — E3, F3, E4 and F4, two slots square — which keeps the grounds at 8 and the house at 44. The outer grounds are now X1 to X4.
    • The Hydraulic Lift joins the Utility line: one door behind an Iron Key, and [Water] [Power] → [Activate], move to any other revealed room in the house. It is the only card in the game that asks for both flags at once, which is the point — the power had four rooms waiting on it against the water's six, and nothing that made throwing the generator feel like an achievement. Now the two machines sit at opposite ends of the grounds and the thing that rewards turning both on carries you across the house in a single action.
    • The tiles are the printed tiles now. Every room has been rebuilt from the physical sheets rather than carried forward from the book: names, copies, doors, icons, locks and wording all come off the tiles as drawn. The box holds 111 tiles from 87 designs — 100 in the Interior Deck for 44 slots and 11 in the Exterior Deck for 8. Rooms that had drifted out of the print files are gone; several that stayed were re-cut, among them the Keysmith's Bench (a key for each card discarded), the Dining Hall (3 actions), the Billiard Room (3 extra actions tomorrow), the Master Suite (3 sanity), the Bell Board and Smoking Room (2 actions), the Coal Cellar (skip the rest of the day's Haunting draws) and the Mausoleum, which now resolves both cards and takes nothing from the keeper.
    • A requirement holds an icon, not a room. Water and power no longer shut a door. They sit in the middle of the tile with an arrow, and they hold only the icons drawn after them: the Bathroom can be searched dry but its bed wants water, the Service Lift searches without power but will not carry you, and the Greenhouse gives its sanity only once the water runs. Where the requirement stands before every icon — the Wine Cellar, the Root Cellar, the Coal Cellar — it holds the whole tile, which is the old behaviour written the new way. A room that cannot be entered says so in words instead: the Dry Cistern opens only with the water on, the Flooded Cellar only with it off. So the only thing that locks a door now is a key.
    • The room lines wear their printed colours, straight from the tile files: Passage 130/130/130, Storage 150/90/40, Utility 0/0/255, Chamber 255/0/255, Sacred 220/170/40, Commerce 255/0/0, Exterior 40/140/60. The two decks are named for what they hold, the Interior Deck and the Exterior Deck.
    • Seven room types, not eight. The Leisure line is gone. It was the smallest by a distance at 7 designs against everyone else's 10 to 22, it owned no icon of its own, and — the part that made it cheap to remove — nothing in the game named it: not a card, a room, an ability or an objective, where Passage is named by four things and Utility by three. Its seven rooms moved rather than went: the Billiard Room, the Smoking Room, the Music Room, the Ballroom and the Parlor to Chamber, which is now the line for private rooms and the rooms the guests were entertained in; the Dining Hall to Utility, at the kitchen end of the house; and the Long Gallery to Passage, where a three-door gallery that charges a sanity to walk through was always a corridor. Chamber and Utility stand at 18 designs each, Passage at 10. Not one tile left the box: still 125 from 91.
    • The day has a morning and an afternoon. Six actions a day came to forty-two a week, which is not enough house for a game about a house. Each day is now 5 actions in the morning and 5 in the afternoon — seventy a week — and actions never carry from one half to the next. The starting player marker still moves only at dawn, so the same player starts both halves.
    • The house takes a turn at noon as well as at night. With seven days and the week ending on Sunday night, only six cards ever came out of the deck. A card is now drawn at the end of every morning and every afternoon, and the week ends when Sunday's afternoon does, before a draw that could no longer matter. That is thirteen a week. The drain, the Calendar, dawn and nightwork still happen once, at night.
    • Night Events are Hauntings. Half of them now arrive at noon, so the old name no longer fit. The deck is the Haunting Deck, and one of its cards is a Haunting card. Every card, room and ability that deals with them says which it means: the Rite and the Parlor look at the top 3 cards of the Haunting Deck, the counter-items answer a Haunting card, and anything that lasts runs until the next Haunting card is drawn — something the table can watch happen rather than a word it has to interpret.
    • "Tomorrow" became "until the next Haunting card is drawn". A card drawn at noon cannot say tomorrow and mean it. Everything that lingers now lasts until the next Haunting card is drawn: from noon, that is the afternoon; from night, the next morning. No Rest for the Wicked is the one exception and runs until the next night falls, since a morning without beds would cost nobody anything. The Billiard Room and the Smoking Room both mean tomorrow morning and now say so, the Mausoleum's vigil covers whichever Haunting comes next, and the Coffin Nail stops one being drawn at all.
    • Dark is now Power. The requirement has the same name as the thing that answers it, the way Water already did: five rooms need power, the generator opens all of them for everybody, and until it runs a Lantern gets its carrier in and a Candle gets one person in once. Nothing about who can enter changes. It also gets an icon of its own, a lightning bolt. Until now it had been borrowing the Peddler's lantern, so a room you could not enter and a stall you could shop at wore the same mark.
    • The Keysmith's Bench takes 1 item, not 3. Three cards for a key was a loss every time: even three commons sell for 3 Sovereigns, and the Locksmith's Counter sells an Iron Key for 2. It is now one card for one key, which turns the cheapest common in your hand into something worth twice what it would sell for. The Bench is one tile that turns up in only some houses and costs the walk to reach it, so when it does turn up it should be a relief rather than a bad trade.
    • Locks go back on the room. A first run at the table showed that locks on individual doors do not survive contact with the board: tiles are turned and pulled every dawn, and keeping track of which side of which tile had been paid for was more bookkeeping than the idea was worth. A room is now simply open, locked with Iron or locked with Gold, and one key opens it from any side.
    • The locked token. Reveal a locked room and you may spend the key on the spot and step in for free, exactly as into an open one. Leave it shut and it gets a locked token; whoever pays later takes the token off and steps in for free as well. The token sits on the whole tile, so turning or pulling the room changes nothing, and it is the only thing the board has to track. The Boiler Room and the Generator Room start the game with one each.
    • The rooms that had gone mixed are whole again. Fourteen that had kept a lock on some doors are locked outright, the five that had a single barred door are open, and The Barred Hall, The Postern and The Locked Landing are gone — without a lock on one door they were a Hallway, an L-Way and a T-Way under other names. The house deck is back to 109 tiles from 79 designs, 38% of them locked. Doors are still coloured by the key, but every door on a locked room now wears it. The Master Key and the Lockpick open rooms rather than doors, and the Lockpick costs 2 actions instead of a key.
    • "Not used up" is off the cards. Eight of them ended on it — Lockpick, Wrench, Master Key, Lantern, Bell Rope, Shovel, Gardener's Map and the Boots. One rule covers all eight and always did: a card that never says discard is never discarded, and the chapter now says so in as many words. Eight cards get a line of space back.
    • Stout Boots are worn, not burned. They were a one-shot that crossed a path in a single action. They are now permanent: stepping onto a travel marker costs no action, and only stepping off the path into a room costs the usual 1. A back path to the Vigil falls from 3 actions to 1, both lawns from 3 to 1, a side path from 2 to 1 — and Jory Nash, whose whole anchor is speed, walks in wearing them.
    • A path step is a Move action and nothing more. The chapter also claimed each step ended your turn, which would have made a two-marker path cost three days rather than three actions and contradicted both the action table and the board's own read-out. What it was reaching for is now what it says: run out of actions partway across and you stay where you stand, out in the open, and you may still be there when night falls.
    • Salt Line says what it does. "Nobody in this room takes anything from it" is now "When a Haunting is revealed, discard this. No player in this room is affected by it."
    • The item cards are drawn the way they print. They were a proof of the layout in the book's own colours; they are now the card itself — a white face inside a 1.5mm frame in the colour of the tier, with a 2.8mm corner radius, black ink, dashed rules under the title and above the coins, and the price in dark coins at the foot. Title and body are both 10pt IM Fell English, and every measurement is in millimetres and points rather than pixels, so what is on the screen is what comes off the printer. The rarity label is gone: the frame already says it, and the line was costing a card two millimetres of text. All 55 cards still fit.
    • Nothing on an item card but the rule. The seventeen cards that do nothing carried a line of lore under the No effect — a spoon engraved with the wrong initials, a candlestick whose pair is still lit somewhere. It is gone. Every valuable now reads No effect. and nothing else, and what distinguishes them is the name, the tier and the price.
    • Seven cards renamed, most of them because the title would not fit on a 44mm card: Pewter Candlestick is a Porcelain Doll, Tarnished Spoon is Bone Dice, Ivory Chess Knight is an Ivory Button, Portrait Miniature is simply a Portrait, Mourning Ring is a Copper Ring, Bottle of Claret is a Claret Jug, and the Mourning Brooch is a Jet Brooch — which is what a Victorian mourning brooch was made of anyway. No title now overruns its card.
    • One fewer Wax Cylinder, down from 3 to 2, taking the Rare Item deck to 30 cards and the box to 114 from 55 faces.
    • Everything is listed alphabetically. Rooms sort inside their line, items inside their tier, so finding a card in the book no longer means reading the whole line to see whether it is there. A leading article is not part of the name for that purpose: The Barred Hall files under B, The Rite under R, and the two dozen rooms whose names begin with The stop piling up in one place. Nothing else moved — the decks, the counts and the odds are what they were.

    The house re-drawn

    • The Last Vigil has left the house. It is no longer a 2×2 room at the heart of the board but a single printed tile out in the grounds behind it, with two doors rather than three. Both ways in are the back paths, one from B1 in the west wing's top corner and one from I1 in the east, so the two players who open it arrive from opposite ends of the house, each having climbed their own wing to get there.
    • An inner garden fills the space it left. Of the four slots freed at the centre, E2 joins the west wing and F2 the east, while E3 and F3 are grounds, drawn from the grounds deck and walled in by rooms on all four sides. No path reaches them; you walk in through a door like anywhere else.
    • The Foyer is one tile deep rather than two, sitting across the front of the house at E7 and F7. It keeps its Stash and the Locksmith's Counter, and now has 4 doors: two along its top edge and one to each side. The bottom edge is still the front door you came in by. The row it gave up became rooms — E6 to the west wing, F6 to the east.
    • The house now stands at 22 slots to a wing and 44 in all, with 8 grounds slots: six out along the paths and the two of the garden. The Vigil is drawn as a printed room, framed like the Foyer rather than like the patch of grounds it stands on, and only its two doors are gold.
    • Ada Thorne moves between grounds rooms. For 1 action she steps from any grounds room to any other that has been revealed, the inner garden included, which makes her the only person who can cross the house without using its doors. Her free movement along garden paths is gone: the shortcut carries her on its own, and she pays for the walk out to the grounds like everybody else. The Vigil is a printed room rather than a grounds slot, so the last walk to it is still a walk.
    • Rooms have copy counts. Signature rooms — the Reliquary, the Master Suite, the Chapel, the Turning Hall — are one of a kind, ordinary rooms like the Closet and the Kitchen are printed two or three times, and corridors come thickest of all at six Hallways. The number sits under each room on the Rooms tab, beside how often it actually reaches the table.

The Cloister is four doors of covered walk, so the inner garden can be a crossroads instead of the dead end it was two thirds of the time. The Graveyard holds two Prayer icons, the first outside the west wing, and gives the Shovel a third hole to dig: 5 Sovereigns and a piece of your nerve. The Folly is decorative and does nothing whatsoever — no icons, no effect, a room that is only a room. The Hedge Maze pays three searches but charges 2 actions for each of them.

The Long Gallery pays two searches and charges 1 sanity at the door. East: The Flooded Cellar holds three searches and cannot be entered at all while the water is on, so the party's own infrastructure locks it and only a Wrench at the Boiler Room opens it again. The Rat Larder pays three searches and takes an item card on the way in.

The candidates are tiles at last — Linen Closet, Cloakroom, Wine Rack Alcove, Wine Cellar, Powder Room, Butler's Nook, Laundry Room, Study Nook and Taxidermy Nook — alongside seven rooms that reach a whole type or a whole wing: The Linen Press (Rest icons in this wing also give a sanity), The Bell Board (an action to every player in a Chamber room in this wing), The Lamp Room (this wing's dark rooms need no Lantern), The Meter Cupboard (with the power on, this wing's Activate icons work twice a day), The Speaking Tube, The Muniment Room and The Keysmith's Bench, which turns three junk cards into an Iron Key. Plus the Back Stairs, the Box Room, and a room in five more lines that does nothing whatsoever: the Dogleg, the Dry Cistern, the Stripped Room, the Empty Niche and the Shuttered Stall. The house deck stands at 107 tiles from 91 designs.
    • The Vigil has two locks, not one moment. Each door is unlocked on its own with a Golden Key and an action, and it stays unlocked. Nothing happens when the first turns; when the second does, the room stands open to anybody who walks in, not only to the two who paid. One player may turn both if they can afford the keys and the walking. The simultaneous naming ritual is gone.
    • Golden Keys come out of a bank. There is no fixed pile to run dry: the supply hands them over and takes them back, and the only brake is what five Sovereigns means in a house that prints twelve all week.
    • The Investigation Deck refills itself. The House Remembers shuffles the discards back in and pays you two cards to choose between for the trouble. Two copies, so a deck of 60 against 56 expected searches never simply stops.
    • Five more objectives — The Whole Set, Below Stairs, Quartermaster, The House Unmoved and Sleepless — taking the deck to 25, and a starting hand for everybody: draw 2 common items, keep 1, and 2 Sovereigns.
    • Cards reworked. The Letter Opener shows you both of a player's objectives, as a very rare card should. Metal Detector draws 2 and resolves 1. Appraiser Loupe pays only on uncommon or better. The Ledger reveals three rare cards and gives you one outright, with no Peddler involved. And the power no longer hands out an action a day — it has rooms of its own to open now, exactly like the water.
    • Nine more cards that do nothing but sell, three in each of the lower tiers and one rare, plus two more counters: Salt Line shelters a room from tonight's event and a Coffin Nail stops the night from happening at all.
    • Night events and searches are counted too. The night deck is 35 cards from 28 designs, weighted so the categories land where they should rather than any one card being likely. Search counts moved off the card face and under it, and the rare-find chip went with them.
    • Two more tables to argue with. The Items tab gained one to match the rooms: every card against the deck it sits in, with its share and how many cards you would turn before one came up. And under the room table there is now a count of every icon in the game — how many exist in each deck and how many reach the table once 42 rooms are dealt indoors and 8 outside. That last column is the one worth staring at: 56 Investigate icons in an average house against an Investigation Deck of 58, seven and a bit beds, six prayers, four Stashes.

Staples come thickest — four Cold Suppers, four Smelling Salts, three Candles, three Wrenches — tools sit at two or three so they are findable without being certain, the sell-only pieces pad the draw at three apiece, and anything that changes a whole game is the only one of itself: the Master Key, the Pocket Watch, the Coin Purse, the Torn Blueprint. The Common Item Deck is 71 cards, 52 common against 19 uncommon; the Rare Item Deck is 27, 21 against 6. That is the three-to-one the chapter always claimed and never had.

Door Wedge is the answer to somebody else's nightwork — discard it as they turn or pull a room and the room stays exactly as it was. Bedroll makes the room you are standing in a bed for one night, which is the first answer to a house that may deal its bedrooms anywhere. Prayer Book puts two Shielded charges outside the Sacred line, and Calling Card trades a look at one of your objectives for a look at one of theirs.
    • The boiler and the generator are printed on the board. They stand at C4 and H4, one to a wing, each with a single door facing the house and an Iron Key rather than a Golden one. Drawn from the deck they turned up 41% of the time apiece and both together only 17%, which left the water unreachable in a third of games and stranded the seven rooms that wait on it — and one game in five had no route to either flag all week. Machinery is the one thing in this house that is bolted to the building, so it no longer shuffles. Dropping them to an Iron Key matters as much as printing them: two guaranteed gold doors would have competed with the Vigil's two, and "always there" would have quietly meant "never opened".
    • Room slots fall to 44, 22 to a wing, and the house deck to 114 tiles. The box holds 130 tiles from 94 designs.

Each has been given something of its own: the Back Stairs and Butler's Nook take two searches and a key rather than shadowing the T-Way, the Laundry Room and Powder Room answer to the water they plainly need, the Study Nook trades a search for a look at somebody's objective, the Cloakroom becomes a place to leave things rather than search them, the Taxidermy Nook fills to three searches, and the Box Room keeps the bed and gives up the search. The five rooms that do nothing now each do nothing in a different shape, from the Folly's single door to the Shuttered Stall standing open on all four sides.

Its hatch lifts on pressure, so it stays sealed until the party turns the water on — and then it is an empty tank with nothing in it at all. One room in the line shuts when the water comes and the other opens, and neither gives you a thing for it.

One Stash icon indoors was never enough — most games the only one in the mansion was the Foyer's, which made carrying anything anywhere a walk to the front door. The Cloakroom keeps things rather than hiding them, The Dressing Room joins the Chamber line and The Still Room the Utility line, and the Dumbwaiter gains one, since a shaft that carries things between floors is the obvious place to leave some. Five rooms in four lines, from 1.3 expected in play to 3.3.

A dark room used to want a Lantern and nothing else, which meant one lamp for the whole party and a lot of passing it back through the Stash. Switch the power on and every dark room in the house opens for everyone at once; until then a Lantern still carries its holder through, and a Candle still gets one person through once. It is the only requirement in the game answered by either of two things, and it is why a draw can never seal a cellar for good. The Lantern drops to uncommon, since it is a way in rather than the only way, and the Lamp Room now spares its wing both — no light, no power, walk in.

The Wrench, the Shovel and the Bell Rope now do their work and stay in your hand, and a new uncommon, the Gardener's Map, joins the Shovel as the second half of what The Rose Bed wants. The Clock Room answers for itself instead — turning the Calendar spends that icon for good, so no stack of Wrenches can wind the week back forever.

    • Locks mean something now. A key is the price of a better room rather than a tax on an ordinary one, and Chapter VII carries the ladder: one search or nothing is open, two or three searches wants an Iron Key, so does a four-door junction or a corridor that does more than carry you, and the room that defines a line still wants a Golden one. The Closet, Pantry, Grand Staircase, Turning Hall, Secret Passage, Dining Hall, Billiard Room, Shrine, Ossuary and Chantry all went behind an Iron Key, taking the house deck from 26% locked to 44%. The Investigation Deck now holds four Iron Keys rather than two to pay for it.

The Rat Larder and the Deadfall keep three searches apiece and stay open, because they already charge at the door in items and in blood — and the same goes for anything gated by water, by a Lantern, or by the tool its Activate waits on. Commerce is still never locked at any tier.

The house deck is 78 tiles for 46 slots, the grounds 16 for 8, and the box holds 94 tiles from 68 designs, seven fewer than before while a one-of-a-kind room now appears more often, not less: 59% against 53%.

Storage: The Deadfall, where entering springs three Investigation cards and every skull among them lands on you. Utility: The Workshop, which buys a spent tool back out of the discards for two Sovereigns, and The Service Lift, which carries you to any other revealed Utility room while the power holds. Sacred: The Ossuary, three prayers that each cost a sanity, and The Chantry, the only bed in the wing and a Shielded charge with it. Leisure: The Smoking Room, a sanity tonight against an action tomorrow. Commerce: The Pawnbroker, who is no stall at all — no stock, no opening days, just a counter where an action sells one item at its full price, once a day for whoever gets there first. Grounds: The Gate Lodge, open Saturday and Sunday when every stall indoors is shut, and The Dovecote, which sends cards from one grounds room to another.

    • The Grand Bazar trades Thursday and Friday rather than Saturday alone. Two days instead of one is more room to reach the rare stock, but the window now closes before the weekend, so finding the Bazar late in the week means finding it shut.
    • The grounds deck is now 14 tiles from 10 designs, and single-door tiles have fallen from 64% of it to 36%.
    • A table of every room now sits under the lines on the Rooms tab, beside the one for candidates: what each room needs and offers, how many copies its deck holds, how many reach the house on an average game, and the odds of seeing it at all. Every heading sorts, both ways. Nothing is ever certain — even three Hallways to a deck top out at 99%.
    • A lock is on a door, not on a room. Every lock is printed on the door it holds, which is to say the doors are simply coloured: cream is a door you walk through, grey is iron and gold is gold, the same three colours the board uses. A tile may carry one on some doors and not on others. Three corridors are built out of it — The Barred Hall, bolted at one end and open at the other, The Postern, whose side door has been shut since before any of you arrived, and The Locked Landing, one way up and two ways on that somebody did not want taken. The Servant's Stairwell stops being a four-door junction bought outright: two of its four doors stand open and two do not, and which two is decided entirely by how you turned it. The key token stays on the lock it opened, so the board keeps a record of every door the party has paid for and whose colour paid for it. Rooms worth searching still lock every door or none — a cupboard with one barred door and one open one is a free cupboard. It is the corridors where a single lock earns its keep, because turning one now decides not only where it leads but what is behind the lock. With the colour on the door, the Iron Key and Golden Key lines have come off the tiles altogether — off the printed rooms too, so the Vigil and the plant rooms just carry their names. Water and darkness still say so, because nothing on the tile shows them.
    • Most of the house is a mixed lock now. A tile that was locked outright was a flat price and a tile that was open was no price at all, which left almost nothing in between. Nineteen rooms have had the lock taken off the tile and put on some of their doors — the Grand Staircase bars the straight run and leaves the turn free, the Ballroom keeps three ways in and one grand one, the Dining Hall, Storeroom, Dormitory, Back Stairs, Carriage House, Butler's Nook, Guest Bedroom, Vestry, Billiard Room, Coal Cellar, Study Nook and Chantry all charge on one side and not the other — and seven tiles that stood wide open have had a single door barred: the Dressing Room, the Cloakroom, the Sickroom, the Dumbwaiter and the Bell Board. Of the tiles with more than one door, 32 are mixed against 12 locked outright. More rooms ask for a key than ever and the house costs less to open: 75 of its 253 doors are behind one, seven of them gold.
    • The omens are gone, and with them the seventh category. Footsteps Overhead, A Portrait Turns and The Clock Stops read No effect and did exactly that. They made sense when the house had no clock on it and a night that cost nothing was a reprieve; with the week fixed at seven nights, four of the 39 cards doing nothing meant roughly one night in ten was handed back. The night deck is now 35 cards from 28 designs in six categories, and every card does something. The mix a week now draws from: Sanity 8 cards, Choice 7, Resource and Tempo 6 each, Environmental 5, Mixed Reward 3 — so a party sees a Choice night 79% of weeks and the one card that gives before it takes 47%.
    • Card text reads as rules, not as prose. All twelve signature abilities are rewritten in the second person, cost first and effect after, with the commentary taken out: no "whatever the locks say", no "whichever she prefers", no "still safe from him". Pryce now reads "You may enter the Last Vigil through either of its doors without spending a Golden Key, on any day. This immediately ends the game." The same pass went through the tools that survive their own use — Lockpick, Wrench, Master Key, Lantern, Bell Rope, Shovel and Gardener's Map all end on the same two words, Not used up, instead of seven different ways of saying it. The Master Key also stopped opening "any room that asks for an Iron Key", which has not been true since locks moved onto doors; it opens any such door. Flavour text is untouched: it sits under the rules and is meant to sound like somebody wrote it.
    • The Executor does not open the Vigil. He walks into it. Pryce ends the game by arriving — through either door, on any day, for the Move action alone, whether both locks are open, one is, or neither. No keys, no unlocking, no second journey; the Sunday-only, one-door version is gone and so is the two-door version that briefly replaced it. He drew up the will, and the house was never shut to him. All he has to do is get up a wing to B1 or I1 and out along that back path, and the week is over when he arrives. The ending is permanently available to one player at a price the whole table can watch him pay, on nobody's schedule but his — and the Golden Key route is untouched for anybody who would rather not wait on his objectives coming good.
    • Bramwell stops arriving with a worse copy of himself. The Porter's Sack lets you use the Stash from the room you are standing in, once. Bramwell does that from any room in the house, all week, for the same action — so the card was dead in his hand from the first morning. He now begins with a Lockpick, which is the man's own history rather than a spare copy of his job: he kept the keys to rooms he was never permitted to enter, and this gets him into any of them, slowly, for as long as the week lasts. It reads as a different curve from Silas Crane rather than a smaller one — the locksmith holds three fast opens he can trade, sell or hoard, the butler holds unlimited slow ones he can never give away. The Sack stays in the deck, where it is exactly what it should be: one afternoon of being the butler, for somebody who isn't.
    • Reginald starts with a Coin Purse rather than "2 extra Sovereigns", which never said extra to what — the two everyone is dealt, or the two on his own line. The Purse is uncommon, it pays him 1 more every time he takes 3 or more at once, and it puts him on the same footing as the rest of the roster: one card, named, in the colour of its deck.
    • Locking every door is now the exception, and Chapter VII says why. All-or-nothing is a price; a mixed lock is a gamble, because whether you pay depends on which way the tile fell and what it backed onto — decided by whoever placed it. Only the rooms whose contents are worth a key from any direction lock every door at all. The Rooms tab counts them: 23 designs with a mixed lock against 19 tiles locked outright and 5 gold.
    • Everybody arrives carrying something. Each investigator's card names the tool of their trade, taken out of the decks and the supply at setup rather than dealt: Ada Thorne walks in with a Shovel, Tobias Hobb with a Crowbar, Dr. Voss with a phial of Laudanum, Father Hale with a Prayer Book, Mr. Bramwell with a Pot of Broth. The card's name is printed in the colour of its tier, so the line tells you which deck to pull it from as well as what to pull. Silas Crane's three Iron Keys are the only starting gift with no colour on it, because keys come out of the supply and not out of a deck — which is the whole point of the colour.
    • The night stops reshuffling a deck that refills itself. Reshuffled is out. Once The House Remembers was sitting in the Investigation Deck there was nothing left for it to rescue, and a night event that does nothing to anybody is a night wasted.
    • The six slots out past the walls are now labelled X1 to X6. They had been E1 to E6, which collided with the spine cells in column E — there were two different slots on the board both called E3.

    2.1

    Objective colours

    • Objective cards colour by type instead of all sharing the brass accent: Coin gold, Nerve red, Faith blue, The house teal, Duel pink, The ending purple. The colour runs along the card's top edge, through its type chip, and onto the filter buttons.
    • The score diamonds stay brass on every card, so what a card is worth reads separately from what it asks of you.

    2.0

    Thirteen chapters, ten new cards

    • The manual restructured. Room icons split three ways — the icon reference, Rooms that need a ruling, and Tool rooms. Sanity folded into Night & sanity. Trading moved beside the stalls in Sovereigns, trading & the calendar. The last chapter is now The mansion wakes up. The longest chapter went from 1,737 words to 1,064, the shortest from 71 to 236.
    • Eight night events, taking the deck to 32. The Lights Go Out, The Pipes Run Dry, A Day Lost, A Hand in the Stash, Prices Rise, The House Asks a Favour, Keys on the Table and A Warm Room. The house can now reach the Calendar, the water, the power, the Stash and the stalls, none of which it could touch before.
    • Party-decides cards are now 9 of 32, which is where most of the room to betray somebody lives.
    • Two searches added: A Word Against You takes sanity or an item from a player in your wing, and Pocketed lifts a card out of the shared Stash from anywhere in the house. The Investigation Deck is 56 cards across 35 designs, so it now outlasts the 51 Investigate icons rather than running level with them.

    1.9

    The audit rulings

    • Playing a card from your hand is free and doesn't end your turn. The exception is a tool spent at an icon, which goes with that icon's action.
    • Six item cards in hand. Keys and Sovereigns are not items and don't count against it.
    • Anything you hold can be given to anyone in your room: items, keys and Sovereigns alike.
    • Golden Keys are findable. Two copies in the Investigation Deck, and the Locksmith's Counter sells at 5 rather than 6, buying back at 4.
    • Prayer costs no Sovereign, only the action. Father Ambrose re-cut to suit: a Prayer icon he uses is not spent, so it stays open for the next player.
    • The Library and the Parlor come down to an Iron Key. Gold rooms are now 7 of 49, inside the 10–15% the chapter aims at, and iron rooms 12.
    • Objectives re-cut. Unshaken asks for 9 sanity rather than a perfect 10, and The Long Night asks that the Vigil be opened on Sunday, which it could never actually score before. Cartographer and Keyholder up to 4, Lamplighter down to 2.
    • The Kitchen works once a day, closing an unlimited-action loop with the Torn Blueprint. Nancy Pike redraws a skull once a day rather than always.
    • Bars are player marker blocks, not a component of their own, so the colour records who barred a room. Two stand at a time, and unlike a claim marker they don't clear at dawn.
    • Investigators are dealt 2, keep 1.
    • What a lost player carried leaves the game with them, items, keys and coin alike. A Golden Key in a failing hand is one the house keeps.
    • Smaller answers: the Wishing Well draws from the Common Item Deck, the Reliquary cannot be used while the item discards are empty, and unbought Peddler stock clears at dawn with the markers.

    1.8

    Terminology

    • One verb for each thing. Actions and sanity are gained and lost, never restored.
    • Shielded charges, always, and the nightly drain rather than the passive drain, since Passive is the name of an icon.
    • Night cards say discard an item card, so nobody wonders whether an objective is at risk.
    • Two counts corrected: nine gold rooms, not six, and the claim that ties on a night card were unruled — the Night chapter has always ruled them, and the card hits everyone who is level.

    1.7

    One deck format

    • Searches, Night Events and Objectives join the items at mini euro size, 44 × 68 mm. Every deck is drawn at true size, and each tab flags any card whose text runs past the edge.
    • One typeface across every deck. IM Fell English for titles, Spectral for card text. The item cards had been set in Courier Prime, which read noticeably larger at the same size.

    1.6

    Cards that explain themselves

    • The type line came off the item cards — no more Sells, nothing more or Passive · always on. One rule replaces the lot: an item stays in your hand and keeps working until its text tells you to discard it.
    • Peddler's Favor, Trade Card and the Mourning Brooch reworded to carry their own timing, since they had leaned on that line.
    • The scope line came off the night cards — no more Global · All players. Eleven effects reworded to name who they hit.

    1.5

    True card size

    • Item cards are drawn at mini euro size, 44 × 68 mm, with an overflow flag on anything that runs long. Shown that way always, rather than behind a toggle.
    • All seventeen italic notes removed from the item cards. Only one carried a rule found nowhere else, so the Torn Blueprint's effect absorbed it and the Secret Passage paragraph says the Blueprint doesn't make it free.
    • Two names shortened so every title fits on one line: Cracked Hand Mirror to Cracked Mirror, and The Ashworth Signet to Ashworth Signet.

    1.4

    The die comes out

    • The Game Die is gone. Two cards in the whole box used it, which is not enough to ask anybody to roll.
    • Coin Purse reworked: gain 1 more whenever you gain 3 or more Sovereigns at once.
    • Lockpick reworked: 2 extra actions to open a door that asks for an Iron Key, and it is never used up.
    • The starting player is picked at random.

    1.3

    Prices and valuables

    • Every item costs a Sovereign more: common 2, uncommon 3, rare 4, very rare 5. Selling pays one less than the price, so nothing can be bought and sold back at a profit, and the junk in your hand is finally worth carrying to a stall.
    • Ten valuables added across all four tiers, from the Tarnished Spoon to the Ashworth Signet. They do nothing at all except sell, which makes a search that turns up an item a gamble rather than a promise. Forty item cards in total.

    1.2

    The Locksmith's Counter

    • The Foyer prints a Peddler icon of its own, open every day of the week including Sunday, trading in nothing but keys: an Iron Key for 2 Sovereigns, a Golden Key for 6 while the supply lasts.
    • Keys are no longer items. The Iron Key and Golden Key cards leave the item decks, and no stall out in the house sells them. The counter is the only door they come through.
    • The counter also buys any item at a Sovereign less than its price, so a morning at the front door turns yesterday's finds into today's keys.

    1.1

    The Investigation Deck

    • One deck of outcomes for every search in the house, 33 designs: a good share of nothing at all, finds, knowledge, cards that reach other players, and harm. Searching became a gamble rather than a payout.
    • Harmful cards carry a skull, so the risk is legible on the card rather than on a separate hazard icon nobody would ever choose to trigger.
    • The plain key is the Iron Key everywhere in the game.
    • Card wording settled: A Purse Left Behind became Down the Back of a Chair, A Forgotten Larder restores 2 actions, The Stash Remembers is optional, and Shared Discovery, Overheard and Something Watching all reach across the wing you are standing in.
