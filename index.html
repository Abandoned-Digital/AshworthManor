How to Play

Cornelius Ashworth never stopped rebuilding his house, and he never showed anyone the plans. You enter through the Foyer, lay the mansion down one blind-drawn room at a time, and push toward the Last Vigil, a sealed room with a lock on each of its two doors. The house takes a little of your sanity every night. Open both and anybody at all may walk in and end the game on the spot, as long as somebody does it before Sunday ends. Then each player answers for their own secret objectives.

Chapter I

Overview & winning

Ashworth Manor is a cooperative, tile-based exploration game. You play guests of a dead man, walking into his house through the Foyer and pushing deeper into a mansion nobody has ever mapped. The house is built from room tiles you draw and place as you explore, so no two games share a floor plan.

Two pressures squeeze you the whole way. Your sanity drains every night, and the house's resources keep running out: room tiles, item cards and rare keys all deplete over the course of a game.

Ending the game

The Vigil has two doors and two locks, and each takes a Golden Key of its own. They are turned separately: unlock one today, and it stays unlocked. Unlock the other on Friday, or let somebody else do it, or do both yourself if you can afford the keys and the walking.

The moment both locks are open the Vigil can be entered, and entering it ends the game. Nobody else has to be there, and nobody else gets a say. The only choice is whether to walk in.

Who wins

When the game ends, the will is read. Each player checks their own secret objective cards: what you managed is yours alone, whatever the rest of the party did.

Each player holds two objectives, and every card carries a score from 1 to 5 for how much it asks. Complete either one and you keep your place in the will. Add up the scores of the ones you completed: the highest total takes the estate, equal totals share it, and if nobody completed anything the house keeps the lot.

So setup is a wager made twice. Two cheap cards are insurance. One cheap and one dear is a hedge. Two dear ones win the house or leave you with nothing at all.

This is where the semi-cooperative tension comes from. Everyone works toward the same door, but two players who have finished their objectives may want to turn their keys the moment they arrive, whether or not anyone else is ready.

When the house wins

The party has one week. If the Last Vigil is still shut when Sunday ends, the house wins and nobody takes anything.

If only one player is left, the game ends at once, whatever the Vigil or the Calendar says, and the will is read for them alone. A player lost to the house takes nothing.

Allies of convenience

You work together for as long as it suits you both. Or you work along in public and quietly set up failure for the others. Wherever the design can create a moment that needs cooperation and leaves room for sabotage, it should.

Failure is part of the game

A dead end, a sealed-off wing, a lost party: these are supposed to happen now and then. Think of a run like a game of solitaire. Sometimes it just doesn't come together, and there's always the next game.

Chapter II

The house & its guests

Cornelius Ashworth was a wealthy, reclusive collector who spent the last decades of his life expanding, walling off and re-locking his own home, often overnight and without telling anyone what he'd changed. The house never had a complete floor plan, and it's never the same twice.

His will says his heir can only claim the estate by reaching a sealed room deep inside the house, and gives them a week to do it. It also names, by trade, three people who must go in with the heir. They aren't strangers hired for the occasion. They all knew him.

Locks and keys

Cornelius was obsessive about privacy. Ordinary Iron Keys open the everyday household locks, the routine cost of his paranoia. Golden Keys guard the rooms that held whatever he valued, or feared, most.

The Last Vigil

His sanctum wears two locks on opposite sides of the house, because he trusted no one, not even his own blood, with a single way in. Two Golden Keys open it, and he did not care whose hands they were in. What's inside is deliberately left out of the rules. The table decides.

Lost to the house

A player at 0 sanity isn't simply out. They're lost to the house, however your group wants to picture that. Whatever they carried goes with them.

Tone

Gothic Victorian, played straight: candlelight, aged wood, portraits, old-world dread. The nature of the haunting is never explained. The horror works better when the explanation stays just out of reach.

Chapter IV

Setup

  1. Lay out the mansionPlace the board. The Foyer, the Last Vigil, the Boiler Room and the Generator Room are already printed on it. Put a locked token on the Boiler Room and on the Generator Room: both ask for an Iron Key.
  2. Shuffle the roomsSort the room tiles into two face-down decks: the house and the grounds. Everything indoors goes into one pile, however far apart the rooms end up standing.
  3. Shuffle the decksShuffle the Investigation, Common Item, Rare Item and Haunting Decks separately.
  4. Set out the supplyPut the Sovereign cards, the spare Iron Keys and the Golden Keys face up beside the board. This is the bank the house pays you from.
  5. Set the houseSet the Calendar to Monday: the week runs out when Sunday ends. Set both mansion flags, Water Restored and Power Restored, to No.
  6. Choose investigatorsDeal each player 2 investigators at random. Keep 1, return the other to the box, and take that character's player board. Set Sanity to 10, Actions to 5 and Shielded charges to 0, then take what that character walked in with, listed on their card: a shovel, a crowbar, a phial of something, a handful of keys. Those come out of their decks and the supply, not out of your starting hand. From here on, the rules call you and your investigator a player.
  7. Deal objectivesDeal each player 3 objective cards. They keep 2 in secret and return the third to the box.
  8. Deal starting handsDeal each player 2 cards from the Common Item deck: keep 1, return the other to the bottom. Every player also takes 2 Sovereigns from the supply, which is one Iron Key's worth and not one penny more.
  9. Enter the housePlace every player in the Foyer.
  10. Choose a starting playerPick one at random. They take the starting player marker and go first. Play proceeds clockwise.

Chapter V

A day in the house

Everyone shares one clock. A day comes in two halves, a morning and an afternoon, and neither is one long turn per player: each is many short turns going round the table until everyone has used up their actions. At the end of each half, the house takes a turn of its own.

Your turn

You have 5 actions in the morning and 5 in the afternoon. Your action track runs to 10: the last five boxes hold extra actions, from a Pocket Watch, a night in the Billiard Room or a meal in the Dining Hall. Actions never carry over: whatever is left when a half ends is gone, and the track goes back to 5 for the next one. On your turn, take one action, then play passes clockwise. Moving is an action like any other, so every step through the house is a search you didn't make.

ActionCostWhat it does
Move1Go through an open door into an adjacent explored room, or step onto the next travel marker on a garden path.
Reveal1Draw a room tile and place it beyond an open door. If a door lines up, step in for free.
Use an icon1Investigate, Rest, Prayer, Stash, Peddler or Activate. Each behaves differently afterwards; see Chapter VIII.

Cards in hand

Playing a card costs nothing. Discarding an item for its effect is free, doesn't end your turn, and can be done as often as you like on your own turn. The one exception is a tool spent at an icon — a Wrench at the Boiler Room, Coal at the Coal Cellar — which is discarded as part of that icon's action.

Six item cards is the limit. Keys and Sovereigns are not items and don't count against it. Go over six and you choose what to shed at once: into the Stash if you're standing at one, and otherwise onto the discard pile. The Stash is how a lucky room full of loot gets carried home.

The end of your day

  • Once you're out of actions, you're done for that half and skipped for the rest of the rotation.
  • Using a Rest icon ends your whole day, whatever you have left. Rest in the morning and you give up the afternoon as well.
  • When no player has any actions left in the morning, it is noon: the house draws a Haunting, every track goes back to 5, and the afternoon begins with the same starting player.
  • When no player has any actions left in the afternoon, night falls.
  • At dawn the starting player marker passes one seat clockwise, so nobody is always last to act.
Going round the table

Only one token tracks turn order: the starting player marker. Whoever holds it takes the day's first turn, then play goes clockwise, round and round, one action each time. A player with no actions left is skipped, so the circle gets smaller as the morning or afternoon wears on until nobody can act and the house takes its turn. The marker moves one seat clockwise at dawn, and only then, and it carries two other powers with it: its holder chooses when a Haunting says to choose, and its holder may do the dawn's nightwork (see Chapter XI).

Bending the rule

Abilities, items and Hauntings can add actions, take them away, or limit what you spend them on. The Torn Blueprint makes moving into a Passage room free, and that move doesn't end your turn. Locked In stops you moving at all until the next Haunting card is drawn.

Chapter VI

Exploring the house

The Reveal action

Stand in a room with an open door leading to an empty slot, and spend 1 action. Draw the top room tile blind. Every room costs the same flat action to reveal, because every tile is the same size.

Placing the room

Place the tile in the slot beyond that door, in any rotation you like, whether or not any of its printed doors line up with yours. Nothing is ever discarded because it doesn't fit. A room tile only leaves the game when something says so, and one card in the box says so: Imogen Vane's second draw.

  • A door lines up: you may step into the new room as part of the same action. No Move action needed. A locked room asks for its key first: spend it and step in, or leave a locked token on it.
  • No door lines up: the room still goes down. It's visible but not reachable from here yet, and may open up later from another side or through an ability.
  • Doors facing the board's edge or an unmatched neighbour simply don't work.

Paths to the grounds

Six of the eight grounds slots aren't next to the house at all. Garden paths are printed on the board to link them:

  • West and east lawns: from D7 and G7, the slots beside the Foyer, out to the lower grounds slot on each side. That slot touches the one above it, and from there a short path with a single marker runs on to the Boiler Room in the west and the Generator Room in the east. Both machines stand outside the house at the far end of their path, so switching the water or the power on is a journey into the grounds and back.
  • The back paths: from B1, the west wing's top corner, out to the Last Vigil, and from the Vigil on to I1 in the east. Two markers on each, so the Vigil stands 3 Move actions from either corner and nowhere else touches it.

Each path carries travel markers. A marker is a space: stepping onto it is a Move action, and so is stepping off the path into the room at the far end. A lawn or a back path (2 markers) takes 3 actions to cross; a short side path (1 marker) takes 2. Run out of actions partway across and you stay where you stand, out in the open while the others play, and you may still be there when night falls.

A path works like a long doorway, so the rooms at both ends need a door facing it.

The inner garden

The last four grounds slots are nowhere near a path. E3, F3, E4 and F4 sit walled in at the heart of the house as a courtyard two slots square, with rooms on every side, and they are revealed from the Exterior Deck like any other patch of open air. No path reaches them: you walk in through a door, the same as anywhere else. A house built around a courtyard nobody has seen in years is exactly the sort of thing Cornelius would do.

Ada Thorne

The gardener treats the open air as one place: for a single action she steps from any Exterior tile to any other that has been revealed. That includes the courtyard, so she can be at the heart of the house one turn and out past the west lawn the next, through walls and locked doors that stop everybody else. What she cannot do is skip to the printed rooms — the Last Vigil, the Boiler Room and the Generator Room stand in the grounds but they are not Exterior tiles, so the walk out to the machinery costs her exactly what it costs anybody else. She walks the paths at the usual cost too, which makes the courtyard her cheapest way back indoors.

A legible board

You can always see the whole grid, every empty slot and every existing door before you choose. Hidden information lives in the decks, never on the board.

Imogen Vane

Draws two tiles on every Reveal and places either one. The other goes back on top of the deck, so the next player gets what she left, or into the discard pile, where it is out of the game. Both choices say something about who she is helping.

The rooms that were always there

Four rooms are printed on the board and never drawn: the Foyer at the front door, the Last Vigil out behind the house, and the two that are machinery rather than furniture — the Boiler Room and the Generator Room, which stand out in the grounds at the far end of the west and east side paths, each with a single door and an Iron Key at it. A house rebuilt for thirty years can move a chapel; it cannot move its own plant. It also means the water and the power are always somewhere to be found, which the seven rooms that wait on water and the five that wait on light would otherwise be gambling on.

Wings

The house splits down the middle into a west wing and an east wing of 22 slots each, plus the grounds: four outside and four in the inner garden. There are only two decks, though: the house and the grounds. Reveal a slot indoors and you draw from the house deck, wherever that slot stands.

So the wings are geography, not larder and bedroom. A chapel can back onto a boiler room; every bed in the game can turn up on one side and none on the other. Cornelius rebuilt this place for thirty years and told nobody what he had changed, and the deck is built to show it. What the wings are still for is being named: cards reach into one wing and not the other, the Secret Passage only runs within the wing it stands in, and a Furnace Room warms its own side of the house alone.

Locked rooms

A lock belongs to the whole room. One key opens it, from any side, and turning the tile changes where it leads but never what it costs.

  1. Revealing a locked roomPlace it as normal. If you hold the key it asks for, you may spend it at once: the room is open, and if a door lines up you step in for free, as you would into any room.
  2. Leaving it shutIf you do not spend the key, put a locked token on the tile. The room is part of the house, but nobody walks into it.
  3. Opening it laterStanding in a room with a door into it, spend the key. Take the locked token off and step in for free.

That is all there is to track. A room with a locked token on it is shut; a room without one is open, for everyone, for the rest of the game. A spent key goes back to the supply.

Nobody is ever forced to open anything. A locked room nobody opens is simply a wall, and walls are part of the house. The Master Key opens any room that asks for an Iron Key and stays in your hand, and a Lockpick does the same for 2 actions instead of a key.

Two requirements, two kinds of stop

It is worth keeping the pair straight, because they fail differently. Light is a door: no lamp, no entry, unless you spend the Sanity — and that only opens the door, never the room. Power is a switch: the door is open either way, and what you find inside is a machine that does not work. One costs you the room, the other costs you the reason for going.

One pool, two uses

The same Golden Keys open the optional gold rooms and the Last Vigil. Spend a hard-won key on a tempting side room now, or save it for the ending you know is coming?

Opening the Last Vigil

The Vigil doesn't stand in the house at all. It sits alone out in the grounds behind it, and the only ways to it are the two back paths: one leaving B1 in the west wing's top corner, one leaving I1 in the east. It has two doors, one at the end of each path, and nothing else in the game touches it.

Each door carries its own lock. Standing at one with a Golden Key, spend 1 action and the key to unlock that door for good. Leave the spent key on the lock so the table can see how far along the ending is.

Nothing happens when the first lock opens. The Vigil stays shut until both are turned, and then it is simply open — to anybody who walks in, not only to the two who paid. That is the sharpest thing about it: the moment you turn the second key, the room belongs to whoever is nearest, and you may not be.

Walking in is not the ending. The Last Vigil carries one Activate icon, and the week is over the moment a player standing in it spends 1 action on that icon. Anybody in the room may spend it. Until somebody does, the Vigil is a room like any other — you can stand in it, and so can the player who followed you up the path. It has no Rest icon, so a player who ends the day there wakes in the Foyer with everyone else, which is why the walk and the action usually happen on the same day.

One player may turn both, if they can find two Golden Keys and walk both back paths. It is expensive and slow, and everybody can see it coming, because the first spent key sits on the door in plain view.

Turning a key

On your turn, standing on the last travel marker of a back path with a Golden Key, spend 1 action and the key to unlock that door. It stays unlocked for the rest of the game, whoever turned it. When both are open, entering the Vigil is a Move action like any other, and the game ends on the Activate inside it.

The Executor does not open the Vigil. He walks into it.

Both locks, and everything above about turning them, are for the other eleven. Mr. Aldous Pryce simply enters — from either door, on any day, for the Move action alone, whether one lock is open, both are, or neither. He drew up the will. The house was never shut to him. All he has to do is get there, up a wing to B1 in the west or I1 in the east and out along that back path, and spend the one action on the Activate that everybody spends. The Golden Key route is untouched for everybody else, which is what a table reaches for when it decides it would rather not wait on the Executor's objectives coming good.

Who it costs

Turning the first key is a gift to the table: it moves the ending closer for everyone, including whoever is best placed to walk through it. Turning it early with your own objectives unfinished is how a player loses a game they were winning — and watching somebody else turn one is the clock you cannot stop.

A key is not a tax on a room, it is the price of a better one. The ladder runs by what a room gives you, so the open version of a thing is always somewhere in the house if you can find it:

Investigate

There is one Investigation Deck for the whole house. A good share of it is nothing at all, so searching is always a gamble. The harm lives here too, marked by a red frame on the card, rather than on a separate hazard icon, because nobody would willingly trigger a hazard. The best finds are in the deck only once. See the Searches tab.

Rest

Resting late in the day, when you have little left to spend, is cheap. Resting early is a real sacrifice. Rest does two things at once: you skip tonight's drain, and you wake in that room instead of back at the front door.

There are no beds in the Foyer. Every bed in the game is out in the house, one player to an icon per night, so the party's forward camp is only ever as deep as the beds it has found, and the last free bed in a room is worth arguing over.

The Master Suite is the only room where resting gains ground: +3 Sanity on top of skipping the drain. Dr. Eleanor Voss takes 1 sanity from any Rest icon in the house, so a night in a bed leaves her a little better than she was. It costs her the whole day to do it.

Prayer

Each Shielded charge blocks exactly one sanity loss from a Haunting card, Global or Targeted. Charges stack if you pray more than once before taking a hit. The Chapel gives 2 charges for the same action, and the Bell Tower gives everyone 2 at once.

Five is the ceiling. The track on your player board is all the shelter there is. Charges beyond the fifth are simply not gained, however they were earned, so ring the bell when the party has room for it.

Rest and Prayer don't overlap

Rest holds the line against the nightly drain. Prayer dodges whatever the house throws at you next.

Stash

Every Stash icon in the house reaches the same shared pool: deposit at one, collect at any other. Items, Sovereigns and keys all count. There's no mundane explanation. The house just seems to connect these spots, and they are all places somebody once left something: a cloakroom, a storeroom, a dressing room, the housekeeper's still room, the dumbwaiter shaft, and the carriage house out in the grounds. The Foyer always has one, so the party is never without the pool entirely — only ever without one nearby, which is the whole cost of carrying anything anywhere.

Activate

For rooms that change other rooms or the house itself. The Boiler Room and the Generator Room switch the water and the power on, and off again. The Reliquary trades a Sovereign for one card drawn at random from the item discards, and cannot be used at all while those discards are empty. Outside, the Greenhouse gives 1 sanity a day, the Wishing Well sells you a common item card for a Sovereign, and the Mausoleum lets one player keep vigil against the night (see Chapter XI).

Every Activate icon works once a day, for whichever player reaches it first. Put one of your markers on the icon when you use it; like Rest markers, they all clear at dawn. A room with two Activate icons, like the Generator Room, can be used once a day at each.

The Turning Hall is the one room that rewrites the map. Spend 1 action there to rotate the tile a quarter turn clockwise: its two doors swing to face different neighbours, opening a way that wasn't there and closing one that was. It only ever turns one way, and like any Activate only once a day, so a corridor turned the wrong way stays wrong until tomorrow, and reaching a particular arrangement can take days. Anyone standing in the room stays in it; only the doors move. Whoever reaches it first each day decides which way it faces.

The Secret Passage looks like a dead end: one door in. Spend 1 action there and step out of any other Passage room in the same wing. One player a day gets through. It only works one way, so you still have to walk to it, and it never reaches the other wing or the grounds. Using it is an Activate, not a move, so the Torn Blueprint does not make it free.

Chapter IX

Rooms that need a ruling

Most rooms say everything they need on the tile itself. These are the ones that can't, either because the effect reaches another player or because the wording wouldn't fit in the space.

Rooms that reach another player

Most of these sit in the Chamber line, where the guests were entertained. Each reaches somebody other than the player using it, and needs a ruling the tile has no room for.

  • Billiard Room. Your day ends where you spend your last action. End it here and you start the next day with 2 extra actions. It is not a bed: unless you Rested, you still wake in the Foyer, and the extra actions are what pay for the walk back.
  • Dining Hall. Actions gained this way can take you onto the extra boxes of your track.
  • Music Room. Everyone in the room hears it; the only choice is whether to be there. The player at the piano loses 1 sanity for every listener, even if that takes them to 0.
  • Ballroom. The named player chooses: arrive at the start of their next turn, through any door, locked or not, or refuse and lose 1 sanity. A player who Rested today cannot be named: a bed holds its ground.
  • Parlor. The séance is seen alone, and nobody has to tell the truth about what was on the cards.

Elsewhere in the house

  • Library. The objective you return goes back into the box face down, unseen. Keep one of the two you draw and return the other the same way. Like every Activate, it works once a day for the whole table, so whoever reaches it first rewrites their reasons.
  • The Ironmongery. A bar is one of your own marker blocks, laid on a room tile rather than on an icon. Using the Ironmongery, you bar any room in the house, or move a bar that is already out there, off whatever it was guarding. Nightwork can neither turn nor pull a barred room. Unlike a claim marker, a bar does not clear at dawn: it stays until somebody moves it, and the colour tells the table whose protection it is.
  • Sewing Room. Three cards from the Common Item deck go to the discard; the card you draw comes from the Rare Item deck, so it may be rare or very rare.
  • Furnace Room. Everyone standing anywhere in the Furnace Room's wing when night falls skips tonight's sanity drain. Hauntings still reach them.
  • Clock Room. Turning the Calendar changes the day at once, so a stall can open or shut on the spot. It is the only way to move the deadline: turned back, it buys the party a day; turned forward, it takes one away.
  • Dumbwaiter. The cards arrive in the other player's hand at once, and they cannot refuse them. Nothing says they have to want what comes up the shaft.

Chapter X

Tool rooms

Nearly every room type has a room whose Activate icon waits on a specific item from the item decks. Laudanum for the Sickroom, a Bell Rope for the Bell Tower, a Ledger for the Counting House, Coal for the Coal Cellar, and a Shovel — with a Gardener's Map beside it — for The Rose Bed.

Some tools have two rooms or three, and the one you use it in is a choice. A Shovel digs three different holes: 3 Sovereigns in the Root Cellar, 5 and a piece of your nerve in the Graveyard, or, if somebody drew the map as well, a rare card in The Rose Bed. Coal feeds the Furnace Room as well as the Coal Cellar, a Wrench sets the Clock Room as well as the boiler, and an Oil Can runs the Dumbwaiter.

The Lumber Room, the Coal Cellar, the Root Cellar and the Furnace Room go a step further: they are the house's basement and pitch dark, so until the generator runs or somebody carries a light down, the way in costs a piece of your nerve. Drawn before either, each is a dead end on the board, and those with two doors can wall off whatever lies past them. That is the risk of a house that hands out its rooms at random.

What survives the job, and what doesn't

A tool you work with comes home in your hand: the Wrench, the Bell Rope, the Lantern and the Master Key. Owning one of those is a standing job in the house, and the argument at the table is over who carries it rather than who spends it.

A tool you consume is gone the moment it is used, because using it is burning it: Coal, a phial of Laudanum, a Ledger's one night of credit, an Oil Can, a Candle — and the Shovel, which the digging rooms take off you, along with the Gardener's Map that The Rose Bed spends beside it. Those rooms wait for the next one to be found.

The rooms that need Light are the exception to the whole pattern, because the house can answer for them: throw the generator and every one of them opens for everybody, and the Lantern goes back to being what it is before the power runs — one person's way down, carried and never used up. A Candle does the same for a single trip.

Two rooms answer to nothing in a hand at all. The Clock Room spends itself: turn the Calendar with a Wrench and that icon is finished for the rest of the game, however many Wrenches the party holds, which is what stops the week being wound back forever. And the Lumber Room holds only two searches because, lit or carried into, it stays open — each of its Investigate icons is still spent once, like any other.

Both the tool and its room have to turn up in the same game, and often they won't. That's the point: the house is different every time. Three things keep it from being a dead draw:

  • Tools sell. A tool whose room never appears is still worth at least a Sovereign at a Peddler, half an Iron Key.
  • The Peddler stocks tools. Find the room first and you can go shopping for the key to it, which turns a lucky match into a plan.
  • Nothing essential is behind one. The ending only ever needs Golden Keys.

The Workshop is the answer to a tool spent too early, or on the wrong room: two Sovereigns there buys any item back out of the discards, which is the only way a Wrench returns once the water is on.

The Crowbar is the exception that proves the pattern: it isn't tied to a room at all. Discard it at any room locked with an Iron Key and you are through it, wherever that room stands, which is how a party that has run out of keys gets on anyway.

Chapter XI

Hauntings, night & sanity

Sanity

Everyone starts at 10, which is also the maximum. You lose sanity to the nightly drain, to Hauntings, and to bad luck while exploring.

At 0 sanity

You're removed from the game, lost to the house. Everything you were carrying goes with you: items, keys and coin alike, out of the game for good. A Golden Key in a lost player's hand is a Golden Key the house keeps, which is its own quiet argument for not letting anybody get that low. If that leaves only one player, the game ends at once.

Hauntings

A Haunting is a card from the Haunting Deck. The house draws one at noon and one at night, and each is resolved for the whole party as written. Some last beyond the moment they are read: those last until the next Haunting card is drawn.

At noon

When nobody has any actions left in the morning, the house stirs. Draw a Haunting and resolve it for the party as written. Then every action track goes back to 5 and the afternoon begins, with the same player starting.

When night falls

When nobody has any actions left in the afternoon, the house takes its turn.

  1. The end of the weekIf it is Sunday, the week is over: the Last Vigil stayed shut, and the house wins. Nothing more is drawn.
  2. Draw a HauntingDraw one card and resolve it for the party as written.
  3. The nightly drainEvery player loses 1 sanity, except players who Rested today.
  4. Advance the CalendarMove the Calendar forward one day.
  5. DawnEveryone who did not sleep in a bed wakes in the Foyer. Rest, Activate and Peddler markers clear, unbought Peddler stock goes to the bottom of its deck, and the starting player marker passes one seat clockwise. Its new holder may do one piece of nightwork before the day begins.

Where you wake

A player who used a Rest icon, kept vigil at the Mausoleum, or spent a Bedroll as the day ended begins the next day in that room. Everyone else wakes in the Foyer, with no memory of walking back. A Bedroll holds the ground and nothing else: it is not a bed, so it does not skip the night's drain.

So a bed is not only sanity. It is ground held. The party's reach into the house is measured by the furthest room somebody can sleep in, and every morning the people without beds start again at the front door. It also means nobody is ever stuck: a player sealed into a dead end simply wakes up at the door, because the house does not keep what it has not been asked to keep.

Nightwork

Cornelius rebuilt this house overnight and told nobody what he had changed. The habit outlived him. At dawn, the player taking the starting player marker may do one piece of nightwork.

It cuts both ways

Pull a dead end and you free its slot for something better, which the whole party wants. Pull the corridor holding a wing together and somebody is walking the long way round for two days. Both look identical at the table, and the cure for the second is the Turning Hall, which turns for anyone who pays the Action.

Who the house means

  • Ties are not broken. If two players share the lowest sanity, or the fullest hand, the card hits both of them.
  • Room types mean the tile you are standing on: Passage, Storage, Utility, Chamber, Sacred, Commerce or Exterior.
  • Out on a path you are in no room at all. A card that hunts by room type walks straight past you, which is one quiet argument for being caught outside at nightfall.
  • How long it lasts. A Haunting that lingers lasts until the next Haunting card is drawn. Drawn at noon, that is the afternoon; drawn at night, it is the whole of the next morning.
  • Where a card says choose and nobody is named, the choice belongs to whoever holds the starting player marker. The party may say whatever it likes about it.

Surviving the event

  • Shielded charges each cancel one sanity loss from the card.
  • A Jet Brooch, or any other counter-item, does exactly what its card says, on any event. No card in the deck is immune.

Keeping vigil at the Mausoleum

The one room that can hold the house back, if someone is willing to stand out there and choose.

  1. Keep vigilStanding in the Mausoleum, spend 1 action on its Activate icon. It does not end your day. Like any Activate it works once a day, so only one player keeps vigil each day.
  2. Draw twoAt the next Haunting, noon or night, the keeper draws 2 cards and reads both, alone.
  3. Choose one, or bury bothReveal one of the two and resolve it as normal, discarding the other face down. Or bury both, unresolved, and lose 2 sanity. Shielded charges and counter-items can't pay that price.
What it costs

An action every day you want it, spent in a gold-locked room out at the edge of the grounds, a long way from anything else you might be doing. Choosing between the two cards is the cheap use. Burying both costs 2 sanity on top of the nightly drain, so a keeper who shuts the house out night after night is lost within a few nights.

Only the keeper knows

The table sees the card that resolves, never the one discarded beside it. The keeper can wave through Whispers in the Wall because it lands on a rival and quietly drop the card that would have cost them, and nobody can check. Two cards means the choice is always theirs to spin.

The deck mixes six kinds of card, so a draw is never simply “lose sanity or don't”. When it runs out, shuffle the discards into a fresh deck.

No monster on the board

The haunting arrives entirely through the Haunting Deck. Nothing roams the halls, so there's no monster to move or track.

Chapter XII

Sovereigns, trading & the calendar

Sovereigns are the house's currency. They come out of the supply when a search, a sale or a night hands you coin, and they go back to it through Peddlers, the Wishing Well and the Reliquary.

The supply

Sovereign cards are not shuffled into anything. They sit face up beside the board as a bank, along with the spare Iron Keys and the Golden Keys. Whenever the house pays you, whether by selling to a Peddler, digging up coin, or a night that turns generous, take the cards from the supply. Whenever you spend them, they go straight back.

Golden Keys work the same way. They are not a fixed pile that runs dry: they come out of the supply when the house hands one over or the counter sells one, and they go back to it when they are spent. What limits them is not a count in the box but what you can find, sell and afford — five Sovereigns is a great deal of money in this house.

Keys are not items. They never go into an item deck, and no stall in the house sells them.

The Locksmith's Counter

The Foyer prints a Peddler icon of its own. It opens once a day like any stall, on every day of the week, Sunday included, and it trades in nothing but keys, taken from and returned to the supply.

A key left on a door it opened stays there for the rest of the game, so the board keeps a record of every lock the party has paid for.

Visiting a Peddler

  1. Open the stallOne player spends 1 action and marks the Peddler icon: each stall opens once a day, and the marker clears at dawn. Draw the room's stock face up: the common cards first, then any rare ones. Both decks carry tools as well as keys and goods.
  2. TradeOn their own turn, anyone in the room, not only the player who opened it, may buy revealed cards at their printed price, or sell any item for one Sovereign less than its price. Trading costs no action, and only happens at a stall that is open today.
  3. CloseAnything left unbought stays on the table until dawn, then goes to the bottom of the deck it came from. A stall that sells out stays empty until tomorrow.

What each stall carries

The Peddler travels with two cases, the same two decks the house itself deals from. The Common Item deck, holding common and uncommon goods, comes out everywhere. The Rare Item deck, holding rare and very rare, is only opened at the bigger stalls, which is the real reason to wait for the Bazar rather than buy at the first table you find. None of them sells keys: those are only ever bought at the Locksmith's Counter in the Foyer.

Two rooms break the pattern. The Pawnbroker is not a stall at all: no stock, no opening days, no Peddler. Spend an action at his counter and he takes one item at its full price rather than a Sovereign less, and he never sells it back. And out in the grounds the Gate Lodge is where the cart stops on Saturday and Sunday, the two days every stall inside the house has its shutters down.

Selling for a Sovereign less than the price means nothing can be bought and sold back at a profit. Where a discount halves a price, round up.

The Pawnbroker is the one counter that pays full price, and what keeps him from being a machine for making coin is that it costs an action and works once a day, for whoever reaches it first. An Appraiser Loupe adds a Sovereign to every sale and Reginald Ashworth buys a Sovereign cheaper, so without that limit a card could be walked from one stall to the other and come back worth more than it cost.

The Peddler is a recurring, unsettling traveller who somehow always has a stall set up somewhere in the house. Reginald Ashworth pays 1 less for everything (minimum 1). The Appraiser Loupe adds 1 to every sale, and Peddler's Favor swaps one revealed card.

Trading with each other

Players in the same room may freely give each other anything they hold: item cards, keys and Sovereigns alike. To get something to a teammate across the house, use the Stash.

Walking over to someone costs actions you could have spent searching, so “I'll come to you” is a real favour, and one you can quietly fail to deliver.

Opening days

The Calendar starts on Monday and advances every night, and when Sunday ends so does the game. Each Commerce room only trades on its printed days. The bigger the stall, the tighter its window, and every stall inside the house is shut on Sunday — only the Locksmith's Counter and the Gate Lodge out at the wall keep that last day. The Grand Bazar is the sharpest of them: it keeps Thursday and Friday and nothing else, so a party that turns it up on Saturday has found the best stock in the house one day too late. On a closed day the Peddler icon simply doesn't work, and a Wrench in the Clock Room can turn the Calendar a day either way.

Room tiles · 7 × 7 cm · six lines inside, one outside

Rooms

Each line is a family that escalates one idea: more doors, more rest, more prayer, a bigger stall. The top room usually wants a Golden Key, and Commerce is the exception because it never locks. Most lines also have a tool room, which waits on a particular item from the deck rather than a key.

The two decks

Everything indoors is one deck. The seven interior lines shuffle together and fill all 44 slots between them, so nothing guarantees a bedroom on the west side or a boiler on the east. How many of each room is printed sits under its tile below: corridors come thickest, ordinary rooms turn up two or three times, and the room that defines a line — the Reliquary, the Master Suite, the Chapel, the Turning Hall — is the only one of itself anywhere in the house. The house deck holds 109 tiles for 44 slots and the grounds 16 for 8, so a good third of both stays in the box and no two houses are ever built the same.

Item cards · first batch · dealt from two item decks

Items

Held in hand, traded in the same room, passed through the Stash. A dozen are tools, each the key to one room somewhere in the house, except the Crowbar, the Fuse, the Lantern and the Candle, which answer a requirement rather than a room. Numbers to tune

First drafts, every one of them. Only a table settles these.

  • What a Golden Key costsThe supply never runs dry, so the only brake is money: five Sovereigns at the counter, against an Investigation Deck that prints twelve all week. Five gold rooms want one each and the Vigil wants two. Whether that price is the right one is the first thing a full week will say.
  • 5 actions a half, 10 a dayMoving costs one, so this is also how far anybody reaches between two Hauntings. Every card that hands out actions was written for a 6-action day, and is now worth less of one: the Kitchen's 2, the Dining Hall's 2, the Pocket Watch's 1 a day.
  • Sanity 0–10, drain −1 a nightWhether the track wants to be 0–20 for finer events, and whether the drain should grow as the days pile up.
  • Pulling a room costs 2 sanityTurning one is free. Whether that gap is wide enough to keep the hammer rare.
  • Travel markersA lawn costs 3 actions to cross, half a day. If the grounds turn out to be somewhere nobody goes, drop a marker from each path.
  • One weekSeven days to open the Last Vigil, at 10 actions a day: seventy in all, against the forty-two it used to be. Whether that is tight or generous is the first thing a timed playtest will tell.

Rules that need an answer

Situations that will come up at a table and have no ruling yet.

  • Who walks through the VigilBoth locks open independently, and the room then stands open to anybody. Whether that produces a race to the door, a standoff where nobody turns the first key, or one patient player quietly turning both, is the single biggest thing a first game will answer.
  • The Executor as a clockPryce ends the game by arriving. No keys, no locks, one walk to one door, any day of the week — so from the moment the house reaches B1 or I1 the table is playing on his schedule and everybody can see it. Three things to watch. Whether the threat is worth more to him held than spent, since ending it early only pays if his own objectives are done. Whether the other eleven bother with Golden Keys at all once he is at the table. And whether being the only person who can call time makes him the first one everybody else decides to strand.
  • Locking a room againOnce a key is spent the room is open for good, and nothing in the game can shut it. With a locked token as the only marker, a Haunting that puts one back would be trivial to run and would sting in the right way.
  • Nightwork and paid roomsA room the party spent a key on can still be pulled out of the house at dawn, and the key is lost with it. An opened room carries no token, so protecting paid-for rooms would need a marker of its own. For now nothing is safe but the Foyer and the Vigil.
  • Revealing across a pathDoes the new tile go down at the far end while you stay where you are, or must you walk the path first?
  • The night phase orderEvent, drain, calendar, dawn. Written as the order to try, not as a decision.
  • What a starting hand is worthEverybody now begins with one common item, drawn two and chosen, and 2 Sovereigns. It is deliberately thin: enough to have something to trade on the first morning, not enough to build on. Whether it takes the edge off Monday or simply delays it by a day is a table question.

Content still to write

Rooms, cards and lines the game has room for and does not have.

  • What the room types are worthEvery type but Commerce is now named by three decks or more, and the table under the icons counts it. Commerce is deliberately left alone: its identity is the stall, the opening days and the stock, and nothing needs to point at it for that to work. The open question is whether the rest of the targeting is felt at the table or merely present — a Haunting that hunts Chamber rooms only bites if somebody was in one.
  • A gaming roomWagers between players belong in a room built for them. A card room or a gaming table would sit in the Chamber line beside the Music Room and the Ballroom, as a room purely about betting against each other.
  • Negative roomsSix of them are in the decks now, each charging in a different currency: actions at the Hedge Maze, sanity at the Long Gallery and the Nursery, items at the Rat Larder, nothing at all at the Folly, and the Flooded Cellar, which charges the party for its own plumbing. The one candidate still untried is a room whose searches are nothing but traps.
  • More investigatorsTwelve exist and four play at once. Untouched hooks: coin as income, light, the Calendar, and objectives.

Watching in the first games

Not questions yet. Places where the design is deliberately sharp and might cut too deep.

  • The opening nightsNo beds in the Foyer, so the drain lands on everyone until somebody finds a bedroom. A sharp start, or a punishing one?
  • Dr. Voss resting foreverShe nets 1 sanity a night in a bed, at the cost of her whole day. If that loop proves dull, she tends somebody else instead of herself.
  • Beds as contested groundOne player to an icon per night. Taking the last bed so a rival wakes at the front door costs one action and a whole day of theirs.
  • A targeted card on the first nightTies are not broken, so a card that hunts the lowest sanity hits everyone who is level. On the first night that is the whole table, which turns Whispers in the Wall into 2 sanity from every player and Locked In into a day nobody can move. Whether that opening is dramatic or simply flattening is a table question.
  • Keys against locksLocks are where they should be at last — a quarter of the Interior Deck needs an Iron Key — so the open question moves to whether the party can pay for them. Four keys sit in the Investigation Deck, Silas Crane brings three, and the Locksmith's Counter sells the rest at 2 Sovereigns each out of an economy that prints twelve. Enough for eight or ten doors in a week, against twenty-odd locked rooms on the board. Whether that ratio reads as hard choices or as a house nobody can get into is the first thing to watch.
  • Winding the weekThe Clock Room now spends its own icon, so the Calendar can be moved exactly once per Clock Room in the house and no Wrench count changes that. What is left to watch is the direction. Winding back buys the table a day; winding forward burns one, and a party that wants either has to decide who is trusted to stand at the clock.
Nothing on this page is a rule. When one of these is settled it moves into the manual and leaves here.

Change history · 1.0 to 2.2

Changelog

What has changed in the house, newest first. Every other page describes the game as it stands today; this is the only one that remembers what it used to be.

Versions

2.3

In order

  • The Last Vigil ends the game on an Activate, not on a doorway. Both Golden Keys still open the two doors, and once they are turned anybody may walk in — but the week now ends when a player standing inside spends 1 action on the room's Activate icon. Arriving and ending are two separate acts, so a player can reach the room without closing the game on the table, more than one of them can be standing in it when the will is read, and the table can see the ending coming from one action away. Mr. Aldous Pryce keeps his free entry through either door and spends the same action as everybody else. The Vigil is drawn as a printed room tile now, with the Sacred face its two Golden Key doors were already wearing.
  • The skull is gone. A harmful card is marked by its red frame and nothing else — no icon on any card in the deck. Nancy Pike's ability said "a card with a skull" and now says a harmful card.
  • The Investigation deck splits on the page into Finds and Harmful, alphabetical inside each, the way the rooms split by line. Lights Out stops being a dead card when the Power is already off — otherwise lose 1 Sanity. Light Fingers takes 1 Item card rather than 2, or a Sanity from anyone holding none. Discovery is A Way Through and Mutual Gains is Shared Out. And A Locked Cabinet is new: discard an Iron Key, draw a Rare Item card — the only card in the deck that asks you to spend something, and the second job an Iron Key has ever had. The deck stands at 35 designs and 60 cards against the 52 searches an average house holds.
  • The Investigation deck is the printed deck. All 35 designs and 59 cards come off the sheets: names, copies and wording. The cards now print as the item cards do — mini euro, white face, 10pt throughout, no icon and no band — with a red frame on the eight that bite and a black one on everything else, which is the whole of the card's colour. Every filter but Harmful is gone, and a single copy reads ×1 in the deck like everything else. Gone with the old deck: the rare-find chip and the flag behind it.
  • A tier in front of it makes the word ordinary. It is a Rare Item deck and a Common Item card, lower case, while a bare Item Card keeps its capitals. Thirty-three phrases across the rooms, the items and the searches follow it now.
  • The room types are worth something now. A count of every card that names a type turned up almost nothing: Passage 3, Utility 3, Sacred 2, Exterior 2, Storage 1, Chamber 1, Commerce 0, against 18 cards that name a wing. Six of the seven splits were a colour on a border. Eleven cards later, every type but Commerce is named by three decks or more. The house hunts by type — Bad Dreams takes 2 Sanity from anyone in a Chamber room, Rats in the Crates empties a hand in Storage, Weather Comes In costs 2 Actions outdoors. The Investigation Deck pays by type: Servants' Shortcut carries you between Passage rooms, Consecrated Ground and Misfiled pay double in a Sacred or Storage room. Three objectives ask you to shape the house rather than stand in it — A House of Corridors, Made the Beds, The Garden Party. And four items care where you are: the new Floor Plan and Preserving Jar, and the Prayer Book and Smelling Salts, which now pay more in the room that suits them. Commerce is left out on purpose: its identity is the stall, the opening days and the stock, and it needs nothing pointed at it.
  • The Armory splits in two. It was a gold room whose reward was a marker block, which is a strange thing to spend the rarest key in the game on. The Armory keeps the Golden Key and becomes a haul: three searches, the first an ordinary one and the other two drawing a Rare Item card each. The bar goes to a room of its own, The Ironmongery — Storage, Iron Key, a search and an Activate — which marks a room against Nightwork exactly as before. Iron bars are what it sells, and a bar is what you lay on the room. A player who wants to protect a corridor no longer has to find and pay for the gold room first.
  • Every item card reworded. Keywords take a capital the way the rooms already did — Sanity, Actions, Sovereign, Item Card, Objective, Haunting Card, Common, Uncommon, Rare — and no card ends on a full stop any more. Along with it: the Appraiser Loupe pays on anything of at least Uncommon quality, the Lockpick costs 3 Actions, Cold Supper gives 2, the Crowbar stops opening doors and instead gets you through a room locked with an Iron Key, the Iron Horseshoe names the player who takes your Sanity, and the Bedroll stops being a bed: it holds the ground you slept on without skipping the drain. Coal now says what the two rooms say — the rest of the day's Hauntings skipped in the Coal Cellar, the Sanity drain kept off a wing in the Furnace Room — and with it the cellar stops being a way round the walk out to the machines.
  • The Fuse is back, uncommon and printed twice: discard to Power one room until the end of the day. It has nothing to do with the generator now — it is the Candle of the electrical half, a single room lit for a single day, which gives the Power requirement the same kind of answer-in-a-hand that Light has always had.
  • The generator is worth as much as the boiler now. Light and Power together reached 2.7 rooms in an average house against the water's 5.1, so the long walk east was worth half the walk west. Five more tiles put them level at 4.9, and none of them is a new room: a second Wine Cellar and a second Service Lift, and three tiles that always should have carried the requirement — the Lumber Room, pitch dark and stacked to the ceiling, now wants Light; the Workshop and the Bell Board want Power, a lathe and an electric bell panel being the two things in a Victorian house that plainly do. Light sits at 6 tiles, Power at 5, and the Workshop and Bell Board can both still be searched with the generator cold, because Power never shut a door.
  • Light stops you at the door. Power does not. The two requirements now fail in different ways, which is the whole reason for splitting them. A room that needs Light cannot be entered without one — the Power on, a Lantern carried, a Candle spent — and failing all three anybody may walk in for 1 Sanity, every time they do it. The Sanity buys the doorway and nothing else: the room stays dark, so everything behind the bulb is still unusable until a light arrives. The Taxidermy Room is the one that charges on top of it, 1 extra Sanity for walking in blind among the glass eyes. A room that needs Power is open to anyone: you walk in, and the machine simply does not work. Only the icons drawn after the bolt wait on the generator, so the Service Lift can still be searched by a party that has never been near it. One costs you the room; the other costs you the reason for going.
  • Power splits in two: Light and Power. One icon was doing two jobs, and the join showed the moment a machine needed it — a Candle could run a hydraulic lift. Now a lightbulb marks a room that is merely dark, answered by the generator or a Lantern or Candle carried in, and the lightning bolt marks a room that needs Power, answered by the generator and nothing else. The three cellars — Root, Wine and Coal — want Light. The Service Lift and the Hydraulic Lift want Power, because no flame in a hand turns a motor. The Lamp Room lifts Light from its wing, not Power, which is what a room full of lamps can honestly do, and the Lantern and Candle both say Light now. One generator still answers both, so throwing it remains the single best thing anybody does all week.
  • The machinery costs the journey and nothing else. The Boiler Room no longer wants a Wrench and the Generator Room no longer wants a Fuse. Now that both stand at the far end of a garden path, getting there is the price, and asking for a specific card on top of it meant the water could sit off all week because nobody drew one. Each machine is an Activate and an action. The Wrench keeps the Clock Room and nothing else; the Fuse is out of the box, having had one job and no longer having it. The item decks fall to 112 cards from 54 designs, 82 Common and 30 Rare.
  • Ada Thorne reaches tiles, not printed rooms. Her step is between Exterior tiles — the four out along the paths and the four of the courtyard. The Last Vigil, the Boiler Room and the Generator Room stand in the grounds but are printed, not drawn, so she walks to the machinery like everybody else. Without that line she would have been the party's engineer by default, and the walk the plant rooms were moved outside to create would have existed for eleven investigators out of twelve.
  • The machinery moves out of the house. The Boiler Room and the Generator Room are no longer printed at C4 and H4 with a door onto the hall. They stand out in the grounds at the far end of the west and east side paths, so switching the water or the power on is a walk out through the lawns and back rather than a detour on the way past. Their old cells go back to being ordinary wing slots, and the courtyard doubles to four — E3, F3, E4 and F4, two slots square — which keeps the grounds at 8 and the house at 44. The outer grounds are now X1 to X4.
  • The Hydraulic Lift joins the Utility line: one door behind an Iron Key, and [Water] [Power] → [Activate], move to any other revealed room in the house. It is the only card in the game that asks for both flags at once, which is the point — the power had four rooms waiting on it against the water's six, and nothing that made throwing the generator feel like an achievement. Now the two machines sit at opposite ends of the grounds and the thing that rewards turning both on carries you across the house in a single action.
  • The tiles are the printed tiles now. Every room has been rebuilt from the physical sheets rather than carried forward from the book: names, copies, doors, icons, locks and wording all come off the tiles as drawn. The box holds 111 tiles from 87 designs — 100 in the Interior Deck for 44 slots and 11 in the Exterior Deck for 8. Rooms that had drifted out of the print files are gone; several that stayed were re-cut, among them the Keysmith's Bench (a key for each card discarded), the Dining Hall (3 actions), the Billiard Room (3 extra actions tomorrow), the Master Suite (3 sanity), the Bell Board and Smoking Room (2 actions), the Coal Cellar (skip the rest of the day's Haunting draws) and the Mausoleum, which now resolves both cards and takes nothing from the keeper.
  • A requirement holds an icon, not a room. Water and power no longer shut a door. They sit in the middle of the tile with an arrow, and they hold only the icons drawn after them: the Bathroom can be searched dry but its bed wants water, the Service Lift searches without power but will not carry you, and the Greenhouse gives its sanity only once the water runs. Where the requirement stands before every icon — the Wine Cellar, the Root Cellar, the Coal Cellar — it holds the whole tile, which is the old behaviour written the new way. A room that cannot be entered says so in words instead: the Dry Cistern opens only with the water on, the Flooded Cellar only with it off. So the only thing that locks a door now is a key.
  • The room lines wear their printed colours, straight from the tile files: Passage 130/130/130, Storage 150/90/40, Utility 0/0/255, Chamber 255/0/255, Sacred 220/170/40, Commerce 255/0/0, Exterior 40/140/60. The two decks are named for what they hold, the Interior Deck and the Exterior Deck.
  • Seven room types, not eight. The Leisure line is gone. It was the smallest by a distance at 7 designs against everyone else's 10 to 22, it owned no icon of its own, and — the part that made it cheap to remove — nothing in the game named it: not a card, a room, an ability or an objective, where Passage is named by four things and Utility by three. Its seven rooms moved rather than went: the Billiard Room, the Smoking Room, the Music Room, the Ballroom and the Parlor to Chamber, which is now the line for private rooms and the rooms the guests were entertained in; the Dining Hall to Utility, at the kitchen end of the house; and the Long Gallery to Passage, where a three-door gallery that charges a sanity to walk through was always a corridor. Chamber and Utility stand at 18 designs each, Passage at 10. Not one tile left the box: still 125 from 91.
  • The day has a morning and an afternoon. Six actions a day came to forty-two a week, which is not enough house for a game about a house. Each day is now 5 actions in the morning and 5 in the afternoon — seventy a week — and actions never carry from one half to the next. The starting player marker still moves only at dawn, so the same player starts both halves.
  • The house takes a turn at noon as well as at night. With seven days and the week ending on Sunday night, only six cards ever came out of the deck. A card is now drawn at the end of every morning and every afternoon, and the week ends when Sunday's afternoon does, before a draw that could no longer matter. That is thirteen a week. The drain, the Calendar, dawn and nightwork still happen once, at night.
  • Night Events are Hauntings. Half of them now arrive at noon, so the old name no longer fit. The deck is the Haunting Deck, and one of its cards is a Haunting card. Every card, room and ability that deals with them says which it means: the Rite and the Parlor look at the top 3 cards of the Haunting Deck, the counter-items answer a Haunting card, and anything that lasts runs until the next Haunting card is drawn — something the table can watch happen rather than a word it has to interpret.
  • "Tomorrow" became "until the next Haunting card is drawn". A card drawn at noon cannot say tomorrow and mean it. Everything that lingers now lasts until the next Haunting card is drawn: from noon, that is the afternoon; from night, the next morning. No Rest for the Wicked is the one exception and runs until the next night falls, since a morning without beds would cost nobody anything. The Billiard Room and the Smoking Room both mean tomorrow morning and now say so, the Mausoleum's vigil covers whichever Haunting comes next, and the Coffin Nail stops one being drawn at all.
  • Dark is now Power. The requirement has the same name as the thing that answers it, the way Water already did: five rooms need power, the generator opens all of them for everybody, and until it runs a Lantern gets its carrier in and a Candle gets one person in once. Nothing about who can enter changes. It also gets an icon of its own, a lightning bolt. Until now it had been borrowing the Peddler's lantern, so a room you could not enter and a stall you could shop at wore the same mark.
  • The Keysmith's Bench takes 1 item, not 3. Three cards for a key was a loss every time: even three commons sell for 3 Sovereigns, and the Locksmith's Counter sells an Iron Key for 2. It is now one card for one key, which turns the cheapest common in your hand into something worth twice what it would sell for. The Bench is one tile that turns up in only some houses and costs the walk to reach it, so when it does turn up it should be a relief rather than a bad trade.
  • Locks go back on the room. A first run at the table showed that locks on individual doors do not survive contact with the board: tiles are turned and pulled every dawn, and keeping track of which side of which tile had been paid for was more bookkeeping than the idea was worth. A room is now simply open, locked with Iron or locked with Gold, and one key opens it from any side.
  • The locked token. Reveal a locked room and you may spend the key on the spot and step in for free, exactly as into an open one. Leave it shut and it gets a locked token; whoever pays later takes the token off and steps in for free as well. The token sits on the whole tile, so turning or pulling the room changes nothing, and it is the only thing the board has to track. The Boiler Room and the Generator Room start the game with one each.
  • The rooms that had gone mixed are whole again. Fourteen that had kept a lock on some doors are locked outright, the five that had a single barred door are open, and The Barred Hall, The Postern and The Locked Landing are gone — without a lock on one door they were a Hallway, an L-Way and a T-Way under other names. The house deck is back to 109 tiles from 79 designs, 38% of them locked. Doors are still coloured by the key, but every door on a locked room now wears it. The Master Key and the Lockpick open rooms rather than doors, and the Lockpick costs 2 actions instead of a key.
  • "Not used up" is off the cards. Eight of them ended on it — Lockpick, Wrench, Master Key, Lantern, Bell Rope, Shovel, Gardener's Map and the Boots. One rule covers all eight and always did: a card that never says discard is never discarded, and the chapter now says so in as many words. Eight cards get a line of space back.
  • Stout Boots are worn, not burned. They were a one-shot that crossed a path in a single action. They are now permanent: stepping onto a travel marker costs no action, and only stepping off the path into a room costs the usual 1. A back path to the Vigil falls from 3 actions to 1, both lawns from 3 to 1, a side path from 2 to 1 — and Jory Nash, whose whole anchor is speed, walks in wearing them.
  • A path step is a Move action and nothing more. The chapter also claimed each step ended your turn, which would have made a two-marker path cost three days rather than three actions and contradicted both the action table and the board's own read-out. What it was reaching for is now what it says: run out of actions partway across and you stay where you stand, out in the open, and you may still be there when night falls.
  • Salt Line says what it does. "Nobody in this room takes anything from it" is now "When a Haunting is revealed, discard this. No player in this room is affected by it."
  • The item cards are drawn the way they print. They were a proof of the layout in the book's own colours; they are now the card itself — a white face inside a 1.5mm frame in the colour of the tier, with a 2.8mm corner radius, black ink, dashed rules under the title and above the coins, and the price in dark coins at the foot. Title and body are both 10pt IM Fell English, and every measurement is in millimetres and points rather than pixels, so what is on the screen is what comes off the printer. The rarity label is gone: the frame already says it, and the line was costing a card two millimetres of text. All 55 cards still fit.
  • Nothing on an item card but the rule. The seventeen cards that do nothing carried a line of lore under the No effect — a spoon engraved with the wrong initials, a candlestick whose pair is still lit somewhere. It is gone. Every valuable now reads No effect. and nothing else, and what distinguishes them is the name, the tier and the price.
  • Seven cards renamed, most of them because the title would not fit on a 44mm card: Pewter Candlestick is a Porcelain Doll, Tarnished Spoon is Bone Dice, Ivory Chess Knight is an Ivory Button, Portrait Miniature is simply a Portrait, Mourning Ring is a Copper Ring, Bottle of Claret is a Claret Jug, and the Mourning Brooch is a Jet Brooch — which is what a Victorian mourning brooch was made of anyway. No title now overruns its card.
  • One fewer Wax Cylinder, down from 3 to 2, taking the Rare Item deck to 30 cards and the box to 114 from 55 faces.
  • Everything is listed alphabetically. Rooms sort inside their line, items inside their tier, so finding a card in the book no longer means reading the whole line to see whether it is there. A leading article is not part of the name for that purpose: The Barred Hall files under B, The Rite under R, and the two dozen rooms whose names begin with The stop piling up in one place. Nothing else moved — the decks, the counts and the odds are what they were.

2.2

The house re-drawn

  • The Last Vigil has left the house. It is no longer a 2×2 room at the heart of the board but a single printed tile out in the grounds behind it, with two doors rather than three. Both ways in are the back paths, one from B1 in the west wing's top corner and one from I1 in the east, so the two players who open it arrive from opposite ends of the house, each having climbed their own wing to get there.
  • An inner garden fills the space it left. Of the four slots freed at the centre, E2 joins the west wing and F2 the east, while E3 and F3 are grounds, drawn from the grounds deck and walled in by rooms on all four sides. No path reaches them; you walk in through a door like anywhere else.
  • The Foyer is one tile deep rather than two, sitting across the front of the house at E7 and F7. It keeps its Stash and the Locksmith's Counter, and now has 4 doors: two along its top edge and one to each side. The bottom edge is still the front door you came in by. The row it gave up became rooms — E6 to the west wing, F6 to the east.
  • The house now stands at 22 slots to a wing and 44 in all, with 8 grounds slots: six out along the paths and the two of the garden. The Vigil is drawn as a printed room, framed like the Foyer rather than like the patch of grounds it stands on, and only its two doors are gold.
  • Ada Thorne moves between grounds rooms. For 1 action she steps from any grounds room to any other that has been revealed, the inner garden included, which makes her the only person who can cross the house without using its doors. Her free movement along garden paths is gone: the shortcut carries her on its own, and she pays for the walk out to the grounds like everybody else. The Vigil is a printed room rather than a grounds slot, so the last walk to it is still a walk.
  • Rooms have copy counts. Signature rooms — the Reliquary, the Master Suite, the Chapel, the Turning Hall — are one of a kind, ordinary rooms like the Closet and the Kitchen are printed two or three times, and corridors come thickest of all at six Hallways. The number sits under each room on the Rooms tab, beside how often it actually reaches the table.
  • Four rooms added to the grounds, which had six designs for eight slots and nothing but open country. The Cloister is four doors of covered walk, so the inner garden can be a crossroads instead of the dead end it was two thirds of the time. The Graveyard holds two Prayer icons, the first outside the west wing, and gives the Shovel a third hole to dig: 5 Sovereigns and a piece of your nerve. The Folly is decorative and does nothing whatsoever — no icons, no effect, a room that is only a room. The Hedge Maze pays three searches but charges 2 actions for each of them.
  • A negative room in each wing. West: The Nursery holds two beds, but anyone who ends their day there loses 1 sanity, so resting in it nets nothing — ground held without recovery. The Long Gallery pays two searches and charges 1 sanity at the door. East: The Flooded Cellar holds three searches and cannot be entered at all while the water is on, so the party's own infrastructure locks it and only a Wrench at the Boiler Room opens it again. The Rat Larder pays three searches and takes an item card on the way in.
  • Twenty-three rooms added, and the workbench cleared. The candidates are tiles at last — Linen Closet, Cloakroom, Wine Rack Alcove, Wine Cellar, Powder Room, Butler's Nook, Laundry Room, Study Nook and Taxidermy Nook — alongside seven rooms that reach a whole type or a whole wing: The Linen Press (Rest icons in this wing also give a sanity), The Bell Board (an action to every player in a Chamber room in this wing), The Lamp Room (this wing's dark rooms need no Lantern), The Meter Cupboard (with the power on, this wing's Activate icons work twice a day), The Speaking Tube, The Muniment Room and The Keysmith's Bench, which turns three junk cards into an Iron Key. Plus the Back Stairs, the Box Room, and a room in five more lines that does nothing whatsoever: the Dogleg, the Dry Cistern, the Stripped Room, the Empty Niche and the Shuttered Stall. The house deck stands at 107 tiles from 91 designs.
  • The Vigil has two locks, not one moment. Each door is unlocked on its own with a Golden Key and an action, and it stays unlocked. Nothing happens when the first turns; when the second does, the room stands open to anybody who walks in, not only to the two who paid. One player may turn both if they can afford the keys and the walking. The simultaneous naming ritual is gone.
  • Golden Keys come out of a bank. There is no fixed pile to run dry: the supply hands them over and takes them back, and the only brake is what five Sovereigns means in a house that prints twelve all week.
  • The Investigation Deck refills itself. The House Remembers shuffles the discards back in and pays you two cards to choose between for the trouble. Two copies, so a deck of 60 against 56 expected searches never simply stops.
  • Five more objectives — The Whole Set, Below Stairs, Quartermaster, The House Unmoved and Sleepless — taking the deck to 25, and a starting hand for everybody: draw 2 common items, keep 1, and 2 Sovereigns.
  • Cards reworked. The Letter Opener shows you both of a player's objectives, as a very rare card should. Metal Detector draws 2 and resolves 1. Appraiser Loupe pays only on uncommon or better. The Ledger reveals three rare cards and gives you one outright, with no Peddler involved. And the power no longer hands out an action a day — it has rooms of its own to open now, exactly like the water.
  • Nine more cards that do nothing but sell, three in each of the lower tiers and one rare, plus two more counters: Salt Line shelters a room from tonight's event and a Coffin Nail stops the night from happening at all.
  • Night events and searches are counted too. The night deck is 35 cards from 28 designs, weighted so the categories land where they should rather than any one card being likely. Search counts moved off the card face and under it, and the rare-find chip went with them.
  • Two more tables to argue with. The Items tab gained one to match the rooms: every card against the deck it sits in, with its share and how many cards you would turn before one came up. And under the room table there is now a count of every icon in the game — how many exist in each deck and how many reach the table once 44 rooms are dealt indoors and 8 outside. That last column is the one worth staring at: 56 Investigate icons in an average house against an Investigation Deck of 58, seven and a bit beds, six prayers, four Stashes.
  • The item decks are counted, like the tiles. Every card now says how many of it are printed. Staples come thickest — four Cold Suppers, four Smelling Salts, three Candles, three Wrenches — tools sit at two or three so they are findable without being certain, the sell-only pieces pad the draw at three apiece, and anything that changes a whole game is the only one of itself: the Master Key, the Pocket Watch, the Coin Purse, the Torn Blueprint. The Common Item Deck is 71 cards, 52 common against 19 uncommon; the Rare Item Deck is 27, 21 against 6. That is the three-to-one the chapter always claimed and never had.
  • Six cards the box was missing. Sprig of Yew and Iron Horseshoe answer the night: one takes the sanity out of an event for you, the other hands it to any other player at the table. Door Wedge is the answer to somebody else's nightwork — discard it as they turn or pull a room and the room stays exactly as it was. Bedroll makes the room you are standing in a bed for one night, which is the first answer to a house that may deal its bedrooms anywhere. Prayer Book puts two Shielded charges outside the Sacred line, and Calling Card trades a look at one of your objectives for a look at one of theirs.
  • The boiler and the generator are printed on the board. They stand at C4 and H4, one to a wing, each with a single door facing the house and an Iron Key rather than a Golden one. Drawn from the deck they turned up 41% of the time apiece and both together only 17%, which left the water unreachable in a third of games and stranded the seven rooms that wait on it — and one game in five had no route to either flag all week. Machinery is the one thing in this house that is bolted to the building, so it no longer shuffles. Dropping them to an Iron Key matters as much as printing them: two guaranteed gold doors would have competed with the Vigil's two, and "always there" would have quietly meant "never opened".
  • Room slots fall to 44, 22 to a wing, and the house deck to 114 tiles. The box holds 130 tiles from 94 designs.
  • No two tiles are alike any more. Nine sets of rooms shared a shape, an icon count and a lock with nothing printed to tell them apart. Each has been given something of its own: the Back Stairs and Butler's Nook take two searches and a key rather than shadowing the T-Way, the Laundry Room and Powder Room answer to the water they plainly need, the Study Nook trades a search for a look at somebody's objective, the Cloakroom becomes a place to leave things rather than search them, the Taxidermy Nook fills to three searches, and the Box Room keeps the bed and gives up the search. The five rooms that do nothing now each do nothing in a different shape, from the Folly's single door to the Shuttered Stall standing open on all four sides.
  • The Dry Cistern answers to the water the other way round from the Flooded Cellar. Its hatch lifts on pressure, so it stays sealed until the party turns the water on — and then it is an empty tank with nothing in it at all. One room in the line shuts when the water comes and the other opens, and neither gives you a thing for it.
  • The Stash reaches the house properly now. One Stash icon indoors was never enough — most games the only one in the mansion was the Foyer's, which made carrying anything anywhere a walk to the front door. The Cloakroom keeps things rather than hiding them, The Dressing Room joins the Chamber line and The Still Room the Utility line, and the Dumbwaiter gains one, since a shaft that carries things between floors is the obvious place to leave some. Five rooms in four lines, from 1.3 expected in play to 3.3.
  • Darkness answers to the generator now, or to a light. A dark room used to want a Lantern and nothing else, which meant one lamp for the whole party and a lot of passing it back through the Stash. Switch the power on and every dark room in the house opens for everyone at once; until then a Lantern still carries its holder through, and a Candle still gets one person through once. It is the only requirement in the game answered by either of two things, and it is why a draw can never seal a cellar for good. The Lantern drops to uncommon, since it is a way in rather than the only way, and the Lamp Room now spares its wing both — no light, no power, walk in.
  • Tools stop being eaten. The Wrench, the Shovel and the Bell Rope now do their work and stay in your hand, and a new uncommon, the Gardener's Map, joins the Shovel as the second half of what The Rose Bed wants. What is still consumed is what burning it is the point of: Coal, a Fuse, Laudanum, a Ledger, an Oil Can, a Candle. The Clock Room answers for itself instead — turning the Calendar spends that icon for good, so no stack of Wrenches can wind the week back forever.
  • Smaller rulings. The Armory has no ceiling on bars any more, since its Activate already limits it to one a day. The Ossuary and the Music Room lost their Iron Keys, the Graveyard charges 2 sanity rather than a Shovel, and the Gate Lodge now trades every day of the week.
  • Locks mean something now. A key is the price of a better room rather than a tax on an ordinary one, and Chapter VII carries the ladder: one search or nothing is open, two or three searches wants an Iron Key, so does a four-door junction or a corridor that does more than carry you, and the room that defines a line still wants a Golden one. The Closet, Pantry, Grand Staircase, Turning Hall, Secret Passage, Dining Hall, Billiard Room, Shrine, Ossuary and Chantry all went behind an Iron Key, taking the house deck from 26% locked to 44%. The Investigation Deck now holds four Iron Keys rather than two to pay for it.
  • A room pays one toll, never two. The Rat Larder and the Deadfall keep three searches apiece and stay open, because they already charge at the door in items and in blood — and the same goes for anything gated by water, by a Lantern, or by the tool its Activate waits on. Commerce is still never locked at any tier.
  • One house deck. The west and east decks are gone: every interior room now shuffles into a single pile, and only the grounds keep a deck of their own. The wings stay on the board and still matter to anything that names them — a Furnace Room warms its own side, the Secret Passage runs within its own wing, night cards still hunt one side at a time — but nothing guarantees the beds are west and the boilers east any more. A chapel can back onto a boiler room, which is closer to what Cornelius actually did to this house.
  • Signature rooms are finally singular. Passage and Commerce used to be printed once per deck, so there were two Turning Halls, two Grand Bazars and two Secret Passages in the box. With one deck the plain corridors and stalls double instead — six Hallways, four L-Ways, four T-Ways, four Market Stalls — and the distinctive ones fall to a single copy. The house deck is 78 tiles for 46 slots, the grounds 16 for 8, and the box holds 94 tiles from 68 designs, seven fewer than before while a one-of-a-kind room now appears more often, not less: 59% against 53%.
  • Ten more rooms, one last sweep before the table. Passage: the Grand Staircase, four doors that nightwork can neither turn nor pull. Storage: The Deadfall, where entering springs three Investigation cards and every skull among them lands on you. Utility: The Workshop, which buys a spent tool back out of the discards for two Sovereigns, and The Service Lift, which carries you to any other revealed Utility room while the power holds. Sacred: The Ossuary, three prayers that each cost a sanity, and The Chantry, the only bed in the wing and a Shielded charge with it. Leisure: The Smoking Room, a sanity tonight against an action tomorrow. Commerce: The Pawnbroker, who is no stall at all — no stock, no opening days, just a counter where an action sells one item at its full price, once a day for whoever gets there first. Grounds: The Gate Lodge, open Saturday and Sunday when every stall indoors is shut, and The Dovecote, which sends cards from one grounds room to another.
  • The Rite joins the Sacred line behind an Iron Key: lose 1 sanity to look at the next three Night Events, remove one from the game for good, and put the rest back in whatever order suits you. The Parlor peers at the night; the Rite reaches in and takes something out of it.
  • The Grand Bazar trades Thursday and Friday rather than Saturday alone. Two days instead of one is more room to reach the rare stock, but the window now closes before the weekend, so finding the Bazar late in the week means finding it shut.
  • The grounds deck is now 14 tiles from 10 designs, and single-door tiles have fallen from 64% of it to 36%.
  • A table of every room now sits under the lines on the Rooms tab, beside the one for candidates: what each room needs and offers, how many copies its deck holds, how many reach the house on an average game, and the odds of seeing it at all. Every heading sorts, both ways. Nothing is ever certain — even three Hallways to a deck top out at 99%.
  • A lock is on a door, not on a room. Every lock is printed on the door it holds, which is to say the doors are simply coloured: cream is a door you walk through, grey is iron and gold is gold, the same three colours the board uses. A tile may carry one on some doors and not on others. Three corridors are built out of it — The Barred Hall, bolted at one end and open at the other, The Postern, whose side door has been shut since before any of you arrived, and The Locked Landing, one way up and two ways on that somebody did not want taken. The Servant's Stairwell stops being a four-door junction bought outright: two of its four doors stand open and two do not, and which two is decided entirely by how you turned it. The key token stays on the lock it opened, so the board keeps a record of every door the party has paid for and whose colour paid for it. Rooms worth searching still lock every door or none — a cupboard with one barred door and one open one is a free cupboard. It is the corridors where a single lock earns its keep, because turning one now decides not only where it leads but what is behind the lock. With the colour on the door, the Iron Key and Golden Key lines have come off the tiles altogether — off the printed rooms too, so the Vigil and the plant rooms just carry their names. Water and darkness still say so, because nothing on the tile shows them.
  • Most of the house is a mixed lock now. A tile that was locked outright was a flat price and a tile that was open was no price at all, which left almost nothing in between. Nineteen rooms have had the lock taken off the tile and put on some of their doors — the Grand Staircase bars the straight run and leaves the turn free, the Ballroom keeps three ways in and one grand one, the Dining Hall, Storeroom, Dormitory, Back Stairs, Carriage House, Butler's Nook, Guest Bedroom, Vestry, Billiard Room, Coal Cellar, Study Nook and Chantry all charge on one side and not the other — and seven tiles that stood wide open have had a single door barred: the Dressing Room, the Cloakroom, the Sickroom, the Dumbwaiter and the Bell Board. Of the tiles with more than one door, 32 are mixed against 12 locked outright. More rooms ask for a key than ever and the house costs less to open: 75 of its 253 doors are behind one, seven of them gold.
  • The omens are gone, and with them the seventh category. Footsteps Overhead, A Portrait Turns and The Clock Stops read No effect and did exactly that. They made sense when the house had no clock on it and a night that cost nothing was a reprieve; with the week fixed at seven nights, four of the 39 cards doing nothing meant roughly one night in ten was handed back. The night deck is now 35 cards from 28 designs in six categories, and every card does something. The mix a week now draws from: Sanity 8 cards, Choice 7, Resource and Tempo 6 each, Environmental 5, Mixed Reward 3 — so a party sees a Choice night 79% of weeks and the one card that gives before it takes 47%.
  • Card text reads as rules, not as prose. All twelve signature abilities are rewritten in the second person, cost first and effect after, with the commentary taken out: no "whatever the locks say", no "whichever she prefers", no "still safe from him". Pryce now reads "You may enter the Last Vigil through either of its doors without spending a Golden Key, on any day. This immediately ends the game." The same pass went through the tools that survive their own use — Lockpick, Wrench, Master Key, Lantern, Bell Rope, Shovel and Gardener's Map all end on the same two words, Not used up, instead of seven different ways of saying it. The Master Key also stopped opening "any room that asks for an Iron Key", which has not been true since locks moved onto doors; it opens any such door. Flavour text is untouched: it sits under the rules and is meant to sound like somebody wrote it.
  • The Executor does not open the Vigil. He walks into it. Pryce ends the game by arriving — through either door, on any day, for the Move action alone, whether both locks are open, one is, or neither. No keys, no unlocking, no second journey; the Sunday-only, one-door version is gone and so is the two-door version that briefly replaced it. He drew up the will, and the house was never shut to him. All he has to do is get up a wing to B1 or I1 and out along that back path, and the week is over when he arrives. The ending is permanently available to one player at a price the whole table can watch him pay, on nobody's schedule but his — and the Golden Key route is untouched for anybody who would rather not wait on his objectives coming good.
  • Bramwell stops arriving with a worse copy of himself. The Porter's Sack lets you use the Stash from the room you are standing in, once. Bramwell does that from any room in the house, all week, for the same action — so the card was dead in his hand from the first morning. He now begins with a Lockpick, which is the man's own history rather than a spare copy of his job: he kept the keys to rooms he was never permitted to enter, and this gets him into any of them, slowly, for as long as the week lasts. It reads as a different curve from Silas Crane rather than a smaller one — the locksmith holds three fast opens he can trade, sell or hoard, the butler holds unlimited slow ones he can never give away. The Sack stays in the deck, where it is exactly what it should be: one afternoon of being the butler, for somebody who isn't.
  • Reginald starts with a Coin Purse rather than "2 extra Sovereigns", which never said extra to what — the two everyone is dealt, or the two on his own line. The Purse is uncommon, it pays him 1 more every time he takes 3 or more at once, and it puts him on the same footing as the rest of the roster: one card, named, in the colour of its deck.
  • Locking every door is now the exception, and Chapter VII says why. All-or-nothing is a price; a mixed lock is a gamble, because whether you pay depends on which way the tile fell and what it backed onto — decided by whoever placed it. Only the rooms whose contents are worth a key from any direction lock every door at all. The Rooms tab counts them: 23 designs with a mixed lock against 19 tiles locked outright and 5 gold.
  • Everybody arrives carrying something. Each investigator's card names the tool of their trade, taken out of the decks and the supply at setup rather than dealt: Ada Thorne walks in with a Shovel, Tobias Hobb with a Crowbar, Dr. Voss with a phial of Laudanum, Father Hale with a Prayer Book, Mr. Bramwell with a Pot of Broth. The card's name is printed in the colour of its tier, so the line tells you which deck to pull it from as well as what to pull. Silas Crane's three Iron Keys are the only starting gift with no colour on it, because keys come out of the supply and not out of a deck — which is the whole point of the colour.
  • The night stops reshuffling a deck that refills itself. Reshuffled is out. Once The House Remembers was sitting in the Investigation Deck there was nothing left for it to rescue, and a night event that does nothing to anybody is a night wasted.
  • The six slots out past the walls are now labelled X1 to X6. They had been E1 to E6, which collided with the spine cells in column E — there were two different slots on the board both called E3.

2.1

Objective colours

  • Objective cards colour by type instead of all sharing the brass accent: Coin gold, Nerve red, Faith blue, The house teal, Duel pink, The ending purple. The colour runs along the card's top edge, through its type chip, and onto the filter buttons.
  • The score diamonds stay brass on every card, so what a card is worth reads separately from what it asks of you.

2.0

Thirteen chapters, ten new cards

  • The manual restructured. Room icons split three ways — the icon reference, Rooms that need a ruling, and Tool rooms. Sanity folded into Night & sanity. Trading moved beside the stalls in Sovereigns, trading & the calendar. The last chapter is now The mansion wakes up. The longest chapter went from 1,737 words to 1,064, the shortest from 71 to 236.
  • Eight night events, taking the deck to 32. The Lights Go Out, The Pipes Run Dry, A Day Lost, A Hand in the Stash, Prices Rise, The House Asks a Favour, Keys on the Table and A Warm Room. The house can now reach the Calendar, the water, the power, the Stash and the stalls, none of which it could touch before.
  • Party-decides cards are now 9 of 32, which is where most of the room to betray somebody lives.
  • Two searches added: A Word Against You takes sanity or an item from a player in your wing, and Pocketed lifts a card out of the shared Stash from anywhere in the house. The Investigation Deck is 56 cards across 35 designs, so it now outlasts the 51 Investigate icons rather than running level with them.

1.9

The audit rulings

  • Playing a card from your hand is free and doesn't end your turn. The exception is a tool spent at an icon, which goes with that icon's action.
  • Six item cards in hand. Keys and Sovereigns are not items and don't count against it.
  • Anything you hold can be given to anyone in your room: items, keys and Sovereigns alike.
  • Golden Keys are findable. Two copies in the Investigation Deck, and the Locksmith's Counter sells at 5 rather than 6, buying back at 4.
  • Prayer costs no Sovereign, only the action. Father Ambrose re-cut to suit: a Prayer icon he uses is not spent, so it stays open for the next player.
  • The Library and the Parlor come down to an Iron Key. Gold rooms are now 7 of 49, inside the 10–15% the chapter aims at, and iron rooms 12.
  • Objectives re-cut. Unshaken asks for 9 sanity rather than a perfect 10, and The Long Night asks that the Vigil be opened on Sunday, which it could never actually score before. Cartographer and Keyholder up to 4, Lamplighter down to 2.
  • The Kitchen works once a day, closing an unlimited-action loop with the Torn Blueprint. Nancy Pike redraws a skull once a day rather than always.
  • Bars are player marker blocks, not a component of their own, so the colour records who barred a room. Two stand at a time, and unlike a claim marker they don't clear at dawn.
  • Investigators are dealt 2, keep 1.
  • What a lost player carried leaves the game with them, items, keys and coin alike. A Golden Key in a failing hand is one the house keeps.
  • Smaller answers: the Wishing Well draws from the Common Item Deck, the Reliquary cannot be used while the item discards are empty, and unbought Peddler stock clears at dawn with the markers.

1.8

Terminology

  • One verb for each thing. Actions and sanity are gained and lost, never restored.
  • Shielded charges, always, and the nightly drain rather than the passive drain, since Passive is the name of an icon.
  • Night cards say discard an item card, so nobody wonders whether an objective is at risk.
  • Two counts corrected: nine gold rooms, not six, and the claim that ties on a night card were unruled — the Night chapter has always ruled them, and the card hits everyone who is level.

1.7

One deck format

  • Searches, Night Events and Objectives join the items at mini euro size, 44 × 68 mm. Every deck is drawn at true size, and each tab flags any card whose text runs past the edge.
  • One typeface across every deck. IM Fell English for titles, Spectral for card text. The item cards had been set in Courier Prime, which read noticeably larger at the same size.

1.6

Cards that explain themselves

  • The type line came off the item cards — no more Sells, nothing more or Passive · always on. One rule replaces the lot: an item stays in your hand and keeps working until its text tells you to discard it.
  • Peddler's Favor, Trade Card and the Mourning Brooch reworded to carry their own timing, since they had leaned on that line.
  • The scope line came off the night cards — no more Global · All players. Eleven effects reworded to name who they hit.

1.5

True card size

  • Item cards are drawn at mini euro size, 44 × 68 mm, with an overflow flag on anything that runs long. Shown that way always, rather than behind a toggle.
  • All seventeen italic notes removed from the item cards. Only one carried a rule found nowhere else, so the Torn Blueprint's effect absorbed it and the Secret Passage paragraph says the Blueprint doesn't make it free.
  • Two names shortened so every title fits on one line: Cracked Hand Mirror to Cracked Mirror, and The Ashworth Signet to Ashworth Signet.

1.4

The die comes out

  • The Game Die is gone. Two cards in the whole box used it, which is not enough to ask anybody to roll.
  • Coin Purse reworked: gain 1 more whenever you gain 3 or more Sovereigns at once.
  • Lockpick reworked: 2 extra actions to open a door that asks for an Iron Key, and it is never used up.
  • The starting player is picked at random.

1.3

Prices and valuables

  • Every item costs a Sovereign more: common 2, uncommon 3, rare 4, very rare 5. Selling pays one less than the price, so nothing can be bought and sold back at a profit, and the junk in your hand is finally worth carrying to a stall.
  • Ten valuables added across all four tiers, from the Tarnished Spoon to the Ashworth Signet. They do nothing at all except sell, which makes a search that turns up an item a gamble rather than a promise. Forty item cards in total.

1.2

The Locksmith's Counter

  • The Foyer prints a Peddler icon of its own, open every day of the week including Sunday, trading in nothing but keys: an Iron Key for 2 Sovereigns, a Golden Key for 6 while the supply lasts.
  • Keys are no longer items. The Iron Key and Golden Key cards leave the item decks, and no stall out in the house sells them. The counter is the only door they come through.
  • The counter also buys any item at a Sovereign less than its price, so a morning at the front door turns yesterday's finds into today's keys.

1.1

The Investigation Deck

  • One deck of outcomes for every search in the house, 33 designs: a good share of nothing at all, finds, knowledge, cards that reach other players, and harm. Searching became a gamble rather than a payout.
  • Harmful cards carry a skull, so the risk is legible on the card rather than on a separate hazard icon nobody would ever choose to trigger.
  • The plain key is the Iron Key everywhere in the game.
  • Card wording settled: A Purse Left Behind became Down the Back of a Chair, A Forgotten Larder restores 2 actions, The Stash Remembers is optional, and Shared Discovery, Overheard and Something Watching all reach across the wing you are standing in.
